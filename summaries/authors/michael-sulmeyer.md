# Topic: Author: Michael Sulmeyer

## Meta-Summary

The three records attributed to Michael Sulmeyer portray cybersecurity as a strategic, infrastructure-wide problem requiring coordination among governments, technology providers, infrastructure operators, and the broader security community. Two records focus on the maritime domain—especially ports, vessels, cranes, and subsea communications cables—while the third examines how frontier AI may accelerate both cyber offense and defense. Across the corpus, Sulmeyer’s recurring contribution is associated less with narrow technical exploitation than with policy, operational readiness, deterrence, public-private coordination, and the governance of technologies that affect critical infrastructure.

The records emphasize two converging trends: digital dependencies increasingly create physical and geopolitical chokepoints, and emerging capabilities compress the time available to identify and counter threats. Maritime infrastructure can be disrupted through ambiguous “grey zone” activity that complicates attribution and response [record_id:2966], while frontier AI can accelerate vulnerability discovery, exploit development, and attack execution [record_id:2686]. Collectively, the proposed response is coordinated defense at scale, supported by clearer authorities, shared norms, partnerships, responsible technology deployment, and updated deterrence models.

## Research Landscape

The corpus consists of three panel or talk descriptions from major security conferences rather than transcripts, papers, demonstrations, or empirical studies. Two are DEF CON sessions centered on maritime security: the 2025 “Threat Dynamics on the Seas,” jointly attributed to John Mauger, Michael Sulmeyer, and Adam Segal [record_id:1946], and a 2026 Maritime Hacking Village policy panel on subsea cables featuring retired Coast Guard Rear Admiral John Mauger and Michael Sulmeyer [record_id:2966]. The third is a 2026 Black Hat panel on AI and cyber defense featuring Sulmeyer alongside Morgan Adamski, Sergiy Konovalov, and Katie Moussouris [record_id:2686].

Policy-oriented panels dominate the collection. The records frame technologies and infrastructures in terms of national security, critical infrastructure defense, sovereignty, governance, and public-private action. Although technical subjects appear—including vulnerability discovery, exploit development, surveillance tradecraft, port cranes, and communications cables—the descriptions do not provide detailed implementations, measurements, demonstrations, or incident analyses.

The research area therefore sits at the intersection of cybersecurity strategy and operational policy. The maritime sessions connect cyber risk to physical infrastructure and geopolitical competition, while the AI panel addresses how an emerging general-purpose capability could change cyber timelines and alter the balance between attackers and defenders. Sulmeyer’s presence across both areas suggests a broad concern with converting strategic awareness into defensible institutions, partnerships, and operating models.

## Major Themes And Trends

### Critical infrastructure as a strategic arena

All three records treat cybersecurity as consequential because it affects systems with strategic or societal importance. “Threat Dynamics on the Seas” presents the maritime domain as central to commerce and power projection, spanning ports, naval vessels, undersea cables, and illicit activity such as drug smuggling [record_id:1946]. This framing expands maritime cybersecurity beyond shipboard systems: the relevant attack surface includes logistics infrastructure, communications networks, surveillance systems, and industrial or operational technology such as port cranes.

The subsea-cable panel narrows that broad maritime concern to the infrastructure carrying the overwhelming majority of global digital traffic. It describes those cables as strategic chokepoints whose protection is hindered by fragmented legal, operational, and diplomatic frameworks [record_id:2966]. This is the clearest example in the corpus of a digital dependency that is also physically exposed, geographically constrained, and subject to competing sovereign interests.

The AI panel extends the critical-infrastructure concern beyond maritime systems. Its description warns that frontier models can accelerate attacks against critical infrastructure while also offering defenders potentially asymmetric benefits [record_id:2686]. Across the records, infrastructure defense is therefore both sector-specific and systemic: ports, ships, cables, and other critical systems are connected to wider changes in cyber capability.

### Blurring of cyber, physical, and geopolitical threats

The maritime records reject a clean separation between cyber incidents and conventional security problems. The 2025 session identifies surveillance and cybersecurity tradecraft as forces reshaping threats at sea and places commercial, military, and criminal concerns in the same strategic environment [record_id:1946]. Its examples—from port cranes and Navy ships to smuggling and undersea cables—indicate that maritime risk involves intertwined technical, physical, intelligence, law-enforcement, and military dimensions.

The 2026 subsea-cable panel sharpens this concern through the concept of the “grey zone.” Cable disruptions, suspected anchor drags, and nominal “accidents” may create serious harm without providing governments or operators with a clear basis for attributing intent or choosing a response [record_id:2966]. The core problem is not merely detecting damage. It is determining whether an event is accidental, criminal, coercive, or state-directed, then identifying which domestic or international authority can respond.

This ambiguity has direct implications for deterrence. If an actor can disrupt infrastructure while preserving plausible deniability, conventional response mechanisms may be too slow, legally uncertain, or politically risky. The record consequently asks whether new authorities, partnerships, and deterrence models are needed [record_id:2966].

### Capability acceleration and compressed defensive timelines

The AI panel presents frontier models as crossing capability thresholds relevant to both offense and defense. It specifically names accelerated vulnerability discovery, exploit development, and attack execution as offensive consequences, emphasizing that these advances compress attacker timelines [record_id:2686]. This temporal dimension is important: even when AI does not create an entirely new class of attack, it may reduce the cost and duration of familiar attack workflows.

At the same time, the panel description avoids treating AI as inherently offensive. It argues that AI could provide defenders with an asymmetric advantage if used responsibly and at scale [record_id:2686]. The proposed objective is to ensure that AI’s net cybersecurity effect is defensive. This framing places deployment choices, release practices, and institutional coordination at the center of the security problem.

Although the maritime records do not explicitly discuss AI, they describe environments where compressed timelines would matter. Ambiguous cable disruptions and attacks on distributed infrastructure require rapid detection, attribution, coordination, and response [record_id:2966]. AI-enabled defensive workflows could potentially support those functions, but the records do not themselves make or evaluate that connection.

### Governance fragmentation and coordination deficits

A persistent theme is that technical capability alone is inadequate. The subsea-cable panel explicitly says that existing legal, operational, and diplomatic frameworks are fragmented and that governments, operators, and allies have limited options for attribution and response [record_id:2966]. Its questions concern institutional design: where current regimes fail, which authorities are missing, and how new partnerships or deterrence models should work.

The AI panel presents a parallel governance challenge. It calls for staged-release models for cyber-capable systems, operationalization of AI-enabled defense across critical infrastructure, industry-wide norms, and public-private coordination [record_id:2686]. These mechanisms are intended to manage the dual-use nature of frontier models while helping defenders adopt useful capabilities at scale.

The two policy areas differ in their immediate subject matter, but the governance logic is similar. Both involve capabilities or infrastructure that cross organizational and national boundaries; both expose gaps between private ownership or development and public security responsibilities; and both require coordination before an incident rather than improvised collaboration afterward.

### From broad maritime threat awareness to a specific chokepoint

The two maritime records suggest a progression in scope. The 2025 session surveys the seas as a broad strategic frontier, encompassing power projection, commerce, surveillance, cybersecurity, smuggling, port equipment, naval assets, and cables [record_id:1946]. The 2026 panel then focuses on one particularly consequential element—subsea cables—and asks more specific questions about security regimes, sovereignty, attribution, authorities, and deterrence [record_id:2966].

Given that these are only session descriptions, this should not be read as proof of an intellectual shift by Sulmeyer. It does, however, show increasing specificity within the available conference record: from mapping a wide maritime threat landscape to examining the governance of a concrete strategic chokepoint.

## Methods, Tools, And Approaches Discussed

The records describe policy and operational approaches rather than named software tools or reproducible technical methods.

For AI governance, the most concrete approach is staged release of cyber-capable systems. The panel proposes examining release models that account for the increasing ability of frontier AI to assist vulnerability discovery, exploit development, and attack execution [record_id:2686]. The description does not define release stages or assessment criteria, but the approach implies that access or capability exposure could be increased incrementally as risks are evaluated and mitigations mature.

A second approach is the operational deployment of AI-enabled defense across critical infrastructure. The panel’s emphasis on using AI “responsibly and at scale” suggests that isolated pilots are insufficient; useful capabilities must be integrated into actual defensive operations and distributed across organizations that protect essential systems [record_id:2686]. However, the record does not identify candidate architectures, models, security controls, evaluation benchmarks, or defensive use cases.

A third approach is norm-building and public-private coordination. Because frontier developers, governments, and defenders occupy different positions in the AI ecosystem, the panel proposes industry-wide norms and coordination mechanisms to improve the defensive balance [record_id:2686]. The description does not specify whether these would be voluntary standards, contractual controls, regulatory duties, information-sharing arrangements, or formal joint operations.

In the maritime context, the records favor a whole-of-domain approach. Threat analysis should encompass physical assets, operational technology, communications infrastructure, surveillance, commercial activity, military operations, and criminal activity rather than treating each as an isolated sector [record_id:1946]. The range of examples in the 2025 talk indicates a need for cross-domain situational awareness, though no particular detection platform or analytic workflow is named.

For subsea cables, the principal approaches are stronger partnerships, clearer authorities, and revised deterrence models [record_id:2966]. These are presented as answers to fragmented protection regimes and weak attribution-response options. The relevant stakeholders include governments, industry, security practitioners, infrastructure operators, and allies. The panel also foregrounds attribution as an operational requirement: responses to suspected anchor drags or other disruptions depend on distinguishing accidents from intentional grey-zone activity [record_id:2966].

No record provides procedural detail on cable monitoring, sensor placement, network redundancy, repair logistics, traffic rerouting, incident forensics, or maritime domain awareness systems. Similarly, although port cranes and ships appear as examples, the corpus does not describe penetration testing, protocol analysis, hardening practices, or vulnerability remediation for those assets [record_id:1946].

## Notable Talks, Records, And Evidence

“Threat Dynamics on the Seas” is the broadest strategic record. It matters because it characterizes the maritime domain as a key frontier for commerce and national power while showing how surveillance and cyber tradecraft affect a diverse set of targets and activities. Its examples—port cranes, drug smuggling, Navy ships, and undersea cables—offer the widest picture of the environments associated with Sulmeyer in this corpus [record_id:1946]. Its evidentiary value is primarily thematic: it establishes scope and strategic framing but supplies no case studies, claims of observed results, or technical evidence.

The “AI and the Future of Cyber Defense Panel” is the only record outside maritime security and is the strongest source for Sulmeyer’s association with emerging-technology governance. It identifies specific cyber workflows that frontier models may accelerate and articulates a desired policy outcome: AI should have a net defensive effect on cybersecurity. Its proposed discussion areas—staged release, defensive operationalization, norms, and public-private coordination—make it the corpus’s most explicit treatment of intervention options [record_id:2686]. Nevertheless, it remains a prospective panel description and does not document which positions individual speakers ultimately took.

The “Maritime Hacking Village Policy Panel: Subsea Cables as Strategic Chokepoints” is the most focused record. It identifies a concrete infrastructure problem, describes recent disruption patterns in general terms, and links technical vulnerability to legal fragmentation, sovereignty, attribution, and deterrence [record_id:2966]. This record is especially useful for research on grey-zone activity because it captures the gap between recognizing harmful infrastructure events and establishing the intent, responsibility, authority, and proportional response needed for action.

Taken together, the strongest evidence concerns the agenda of Sulmeyer-associated panels: critical infrastructure, maritime cyber risk, frontier AI, governance, and public-private coordination. The evidence is weaker for Sulmeyer’s personal views or unique arguments because all three records are multi-speaker sessions and the source text does not attribute individual claims to particular panelists.

## Gaps, Limits, And Open Questions

The largest limitation is source type. These records are event descriptions, not transcripts or recordings rendered into text. They document what panels intended to discuss, but not what Sulmeyer personally said, whether panelists disagreed, what evidence they introduced, or what conclusions the sessions reached. Because every record has multiple speakers, claims should be attributed to the session descriptions rather than exclusively to Sulmeyer.

The corpus contains no technical evaluation data. The AI record does not quantify capability thresholds, compare models, measure acceleration in vulnerability research, or evaluate whether AI currently benefits attackers or defenders more [record_id:2686]. It leaves open several questions:

- What tests should determine whether a model is “cyber-capable”?
- What stages, safeguards, and decision criteria should govern release?
- Which defensive functions can reliably gain asymmetric benefit?
- How can critical-infrastructure operators deploy AI without adding new model, data, or supply-chain risks?
- What metrics would establish that AI’s net effect is defensive?

The maritime records similarly lack technical and incident-level evidence. The broad 2025 session does not explain how port cranes, naval systems, communications networks, or surveillance platforms are attacked or defended [record_id:1946]. The cable panel mentions disruptions and suspected anchor drags but does not identify incidents, provide attribution evidence, or compare accidental and intentional damage indicators [record_id:2966].

Important open maritime questions include:

- Which detection and forensic methods can distinguish an accident from deliberate cable interference?
- How should governments allocate responsibility among naval, coast guard, intelligence, law-enforcement, regulatory, and private-sector actors?
- What legal authorities apply when cables cross contested waters or multiple jurisdictions?
- Which deterrence measures are credible against actors operating below the threshold of armed conflict?
- How much resilience can be achieved through redundancy, route diversity, repair capacity, or traffic rerouting?
- How should cyber intelligence and physical maritime surveillance be integrated?

The records also do not explore tradeoffs in depth. Staged AI release could reduce misuse but delay defensive access [record_id:2686]. Stronger cable-protection authorities could improve response while raising sovereignty, privacy, escalation, or jurisdictional concerns [record_id:2966]. The source descriptions identify the need for governance but do not resolve these tensions.

Finally, the corpus is too small to demonstrate a stable chronology or a definitive evolution in Sulmeyer’s work. The apparent movement from broad maritime threats in 2025 to subsea-cable governance and AI defense in 2026 may reflect conference programming rather than a deliberate research trajectory.

## Coverage And Evidence Notes

All three expected records are substantively relevant, but each offers only abstract-level evidence.

- The 2025 DEF CON session “Threat Dynamics on the Seas” provides the broad maritime-security foundation, connecting cybersecurity and surveillance with commerce, military power, ports, vessels, smuggling, and undersea cables [record_id:1946]. It is jointly attributed and does not isolate Sulmeyer’s remarks.
- The 2026 Black Hat “AI and the Future of Cyber Defense Panel” broadens the corpus into frontier-AI policy, dual-use cyber capability, staged release, scaled defense, norms, and public-private coordination [record_id:2686]. It is prospective and multi-speaker, so it supports conclusions about the panel agenda more strongly than conclusions about Sulmeyer’s individual position.
- The 2026 DEF CON Maritime Hacking Village policy panel focuses on subsea cables, grey-zone disruption, fragmented governance, attribution, sovereignty, partnerships, authorities, and deterrence [record_id:2966]. It provides the corpus’s clearest infrastructure-governance problem statement but no detailed incident evidence or final recommendations.

No minor or purely logistical record is present. However, scheduling and venue metadata in the records should not be mistaken for substantive evidence, and the 2026 descriptions do not establish that the proposed discussions occurred exactly as advertised. Overall evidence is strong for identifying recurring subject areas and intended questions, moderate for inferring a policy-oriented strategic perspective, and weak for attributing specific claims, methods, or conclusions uniquely to Michael Sulmeyer.
