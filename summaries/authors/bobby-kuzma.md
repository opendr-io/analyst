# Topic: Author: Bobby Kuzma

## Meta-Summary

The three records attributed to Bobby Kuzma portray a research program centered on replacing simplified security models with empirical, causal, and reproducible analysis. Across adversarial automation, red-team operations, and healthcare OAuth testing, Kuzma’s work asks how complex systems actually make decisions, expose information, or progress toward compromise—not how conventional frameworks assume they behave.

A consistent contribution is the conversion of observed behavior into reusable tooling. The DEF CON work proposes tooling for deterministic selection of adversarial tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) [record_id:2056]. The red-team study turns detailed command histories into causal knowledge graphs and releases the analysis framework as Ithildin [record_id:2722]. The healthcare API research packages a six-phase testing methodology and a 22-module toolkit, FHIRbug, for reproducing cross-vendor OAuth and FHIR assessments [record_id:2723].

The collection is small and consists of conference descriptions rather than complete papers, datasets, or transcripts. Nevertheless, it supports several strong themes: skepticism toward linear abstractions, emphasis on decision-making and information flow, measurement across multiple targets or engagements, interest in artifacts generated by failure, and a preference for open-source research outputs.

## Research Landscape

The records cover three conference talks spanning 2025–2026. One is a jointly authored DEF CON 33 presentation on adversarial automation and AI-related decision-making [record_id:2056]. The other two are Bobby Kuzma talks scheduled for BSidesLV 2026: an empirical analysis of 95 red-team engagements [record_id:2722] and a cross-vendor investigation of OAuth behavior in production healthcare FHIR APIs [record_id:2723].

Although the application areas differ, the research landscape is broadly defensive and measurement-driven:

- **Adversarial automation:** examining the contrast between probabilistic generative systems and deterministic adversarial strategies [record_id:2056].
- **Detection and red-team telemetry:** reconstructing how operators move between actions, what they learn, and which failures create under-monitored evidence [record_id:2722].
- **Identity and application security:** identifying response discrepancies that reveal OAuth client identifiers across healthcare platforms [record_id:2723].

The records are dominated by presentation abstracts that announce findings, methods, and planned or released tools. They do not provide the underlying terminal logs, causal graph construction details, source code contents, statistical analysis, full vulnerability reports, or talk transcripts. Thus, the collection is strongest as evidence of research scope, claimed findings, and methodological direction. It is weaker as independent validation of those findings.

The work also varies in maturity and evidentiary style. The adversarial automation talk is conceptual and forward-looking, describing a tension between probabilistic and deterministic systems and promising tooling [record_id:2056]. The two 2026 talks make substantially more concrete empirical claims, including sample sizes, rates, observed patterns, and named implementation classes [record_id:2722] [record_id:2723].

## Major Themes And Trends

### Security behavior is not adequately represented by simple linear models

The clearest recurring theme is resistance to reductive models of adversarial behavior. In the red-team study, Kuzma directly argues that operators do not follow a conventional kill chain. Based on 95 engagements, the record says operators repeatedly spiral between credential access and discovery—an average of 12 times per engagement—rather than progressing through a clean sequence of stages [record_id:2722]. This reframes intrusion activity as iterative, contingent, and knowledge-driven.

The adversarial automation talk addresses a related modeling problem. It contrasts “stochastic parrots,” or generative systems producing probabilistic outputs, with “deterministic predators,” which select predictable adversarial behavior [record_id:2056]. While this record does not present the same operational dataset, both talks focus on the mechanics underlying action selection. Together, they suggest that useful security models must explain how an actor or automated system chooses its next action under uncertainty, rather than merely labeling a sequence after the fact.

The healthcare research challenges another simplifying assumption: that separate deployments of a nominally standard OAuth 2.0 and SMART-on-FHIR stack will behave consistently and securely. The record instead reports product- and implementation-specific response discrepancies across 14 tested stacks [record_id:2723]. Standards compliance and shared architecture do not eliminate variation at the authentication layer.

### Knowledge flow is treated as a first-class security variable

The red-team study’s distinctive analytical move is to represent operator actions through causal knowledge graphs. These graphs track how knowledge moves between actions, rather than treating commands as an unstructured chronological list [record_id:2722]. Several reported findings follow from that model:

- Hostnames, rather than IP addresses, are described as the principal knowledge bottleneck.
- One-third of engagements reportedly pivot on a single breakthrough command.
- Causal edge density is claimed to predict reaching exploitation better than raw command count [record_id:2722].

This focus on information acquisition also connects to the OAuth work. Client ID enumeration is itself an information leak: differing error responses allow an observer to infer whether a client identifier exists [record_id:2723]. In both contexts, seemingly small pieces of knowledge can alter the next available actions. A hostname can enable or focus a red-team pivot; confirmation of a valid OAuth client ID can reduce uncertainty during authentication-layer testing.

The adversarial automation record provides the conceptual umbrella for this concern. Decision-making under uncertainty depends on what a system knows, how it evaluates possible actions, and whether action selection is probabilistic or deterministic [record_id:2056].

### Failure is productive and observable

A second notable theme is that unsuccessful actions can still create meaningful security evidence. In the 95-engagement study, lateral movement reportedly fails 58% of the time, with most failures generating artifacts that defenders do not monitor [record_id:2722]. This challenges detection strategies that focus primarily on successful compromise or known attack-stage transitions.

The healthcare testing record shows a different use of discrepancies and corrections. OAuth error-response differences become the signal used to infer client ID validity [record_id:2723]. The research also reports a mid-engagement correction involving a CMS DPC bulk-FHIR serialization leak: an initially HAPI-like behavior was ultimately attributed to DPC’s non-HAPI serializer rather than HAPI itself [record_id:2723]. That correction is important because it demonstrates that similar output patterns do not necessarily establish a common implementation cause.

Collectively, the records encourage analysts to inspect failures, errors, and anomalies as evidence. They also imply that attribution should remain revisable when deeper technical investigation contradicts an initial hypothesis.

### Reproducibility and reusable tooling are recurring deliverables

Every record mentions tooling or a reusable methodology:

- A deterministic TTP selection engine is associated with the adversarial automation presentation [record_id:2056].
- Ithildin is announced as an open-source toolkit for applying causal analysis to other red-team engagements [record_id:2722].
- FHIRbug is described as a 22-module, 14-subcommand toolkit, accompanied by a six-phase `PLAYBOOK.md` methodology [record_id:2723].

This is more than a pattern of releasing code. Each tool corresponds to a particular analytical abstraction: deterministic action selection, causal graphs of operator knowledge, or structured cross-vendor FHIR/OAuth testing. The tooling is intended to let other practitioners reproduce or adapt the research rather than consume only its conclusions.

### The work trends from broad conceptual framing toward tightly scoped empirical studies

Chronologically, the 2025 DEF CON talk is broad, covering game-playing AI, cybersecurity defenses, algorithms, and the future of adversarial automation [record_id:2056]. The 2026 records are narrower and more heavily quantified. One draws from 1,265 terminal sessions and 6,001 commands across 95 red-team engagements [record_id:2722]. The other describes a 72-hour engagement involving at least 14 healthcare OAuth implementations, a 16-stack discovery survey, ten JWT attack classes, and a 100-endpoint follow-up sample [record_id:2723].

This may indicate a shift toward larger observational datasets and cross-target comparative testing. Given only three records, however, it is safer to describe this as a difference among talks rather than a definitive evolution in Kuzma’s overall research agenda.

## Methods, Tools, And Approaches Discussed

### Deterministic adversarial TTP selection

The DEF CON presentation, coauthored with Michael Odell, frames adversarial automation as a decision problem. It distinguishes generative models that produce probabilistic outputs from systems engineered to choose predictable adversarial strategies [record_id:2056]. The talk promises tooling around a “deterministic TTP selection engine,” suggesting an approach in which adversarial actions are selected through explicit rules, state, or decision logic rather than unconstrained generative output.

The abstract does not specify the engine’s architecture, inputs, scoring criteria, TTP representation, or relationship to frameworks such as MITRE ATT&CK. It also does not establish whether the engine is designed for red-team simulation, defensive testing, automated response, or multiple uses. Its main methodological contribution in the available evidence is the framing of adversarial automation around controllable decision behavior under uncertainty [record_id:2056].

### Causal knowledge graphs for red-team analysis

The red-team project records terminal activity at a detailed level: 1,265 sessions and 6,001 commands from 95 engagements [record_id:2722]. Rather than analyzing only command frequency or ordering, it constructs causal knowledge graphs intended to represent how operator knowledge flows between actions.

This method appears designed to distinguish mere temporal adjacency from operational dependency. For example, a discovery command may reveal a hostname, which then enables credential use or a lateral-movement attempt. The claimed importance of causal edge density suggests the graph’s connectivity captures meaningful progress better than total activity volume [record_id:2722].

Ithildin is the open-source implementation released so others can apply the analysis to their own engagement data [record_id:2722]. The abstract does not explain how causal edges are inferred, whether they are manually labeled or automatically generated, how sensitive information is handled, or how engagement outcomes are defined. Those details are critical for assessing the method’s validity and portability.

### Cross-vendor differential testing of OAuth responses

The FHIRbug study uses response discrepancies to identify OAuth client ID enumeration. It tests more than 14 healthcare FHIR OAuth implementations for a single bug class associated with CWE-204 and a claimed gap around OAuth error handling in RFC 6749 §5.2 [record_id:2723]. The method compares error responses to determine whether client identifier existence can be inferred.

The record reports four error-discriminator patterns across seven of 14 stacks. Three affected implementations are identified as distinct OAuth products: Okta, IBM Security Verify, and Django OAuth Toolkit. A fourth is described as a major EHR vendor whose product code affects more than 748 hospital deployments [record_id:2723].

To test whether the latter behavior was a product-level defect rather than isolated configuration drift, the study expanded to a random sample of 100 endpoints. It reports that 98 of 100 produced cryptographically byte-identical responses within response classes, assessed through SHA-256 hashes across independent hospital deployments [record_id:2723]. This breadth test is the collection’s most explicit example of using response normalization and hashing to support a cross-deployment inference.

The record also names apparently non-vulnerable comparison stacks—CMS BCDA’s Go SSAS, Redox Auth0-plus-Okta tenants, and Microsoft Entra—and argues that these counterexamples show the enumeration pattern is avoidable through consistent error-response hygiene [record_id:2723].

### Broader FHIR assessment workflow

The FHIR engagement extends beyond client ID enumeration. It includes a discovery survey across 16 SMART-on-FHIR stacks, JWT fuzzing with ten attack classes, and an investigation of CMS DPC bulk-FHIR serialization behavior [record_id:2723]. The resulting FHIRbug toolkit contains 22 modules and 14 command-line subcommands, while `PLAYBOOK.md` documents a six-phase methodology intended for reuse.

The record also describes coordinated-disclosure constraints. Findings involving vendors still inside an active disclosure window are discussed only in aggregate, with details to be added if the 90-day window has closed by the conference date [record_id:2723]. This approach balances reproducibility and public reporting against unresolved vendor exposure, though it limits independent scrutiny of some claims.

## Notable Talks, Records, And Evidence

### “Your Red Team Doesn’t Follow a Kill Chain”

This record is the strongest source for Kuzma’s empirical analysis of offensive operations [record_id:2722]. Its importance lies in both scale and conceptual challenge. Ninety-five engagements, 1,265 sessions, and 6,001 commands provide a substantial observational basis relative to the other records. The findings directly contest widely used linear attack models and replace them with an iterative, causally connected view of operator behavior.

The most notable claims are the average of 12 spirals between credential access and discovery, a 58% lateral-movement failure rate, the under-monitoring of failure artifacts, the importance of hostnames as knowledge bottlenecks, and the predictive value of causal edge density [record_id:2722]. These findings could have implications for detection engineering, telemetry design, red-team reporting, and the evaluation of automated attack systems.

However, the abstract gives no confidence intervals, engagement selection criteria, customer or environment breakdown, operator composition, or baseline comparison. The reported percentages and predictors should therefore be treated as claims from the talk rather than universally established results.

### “FHIRbug: Cross-Vendor OAuth Security Patterns in 14 Production Healthcare APIs”

This is the most technically detailed and sector-specific record [record_id:2723]. It combines regulatory context, vulnerability discovery, cross-vendor comparison, breadth testing, tooling, and disclosure practice. The study is especially notable for attempting to distinguish isolated misconfiguration from a shared product defect by testing 100 endpoints and comparing hashed responses.

The report of seven vulnerable stacks out of 14 provides a clear headline, but the more significant contribution may be the comparative design. Vulnerable and non-vulnerable implementations are considered together, enabling the researcher to argue that secure error behavior is feasible rather than merely aspirational [record_id:2723]. The explicit correction of the CMS DPC serialization attribution is also methodologically valuable because it documents a case where resemblance to a known framework did not establish causation.

Evidence is still limited by the abstract format. The raw request structures, response classes, controls, sampling procedure, and practical exploitability are not shown. Some vendors remain unnamed due to coordinated disclosure [record_id:2723].

### “Decision Making in Adversarial Automation”

The DEF CON 33 talk is the broadest conceptual record and the only coauthored work in the set [record_id:2056]. It matters because it frames the underlying decision problem that resonates with the later operational research: security systems and adversaries must choose actions in uncertain, competitive environments.

Its “stochastic parrots” versus “deterministic predators” contrast offers a memorable taxonomy for thinking about generative AI and purpose-built adversarial systems [record_id:2056]. The planned deterministic TTP selection engine could provide a bridge between AI-security discourse and reproducible attack simulation. Yet the available description supplies no experimental results or implementation details, making this the weakest of the three records as evidence for concrete technical performance.

## Gaps, Limits, And Open Questions

The most important limitation is source depth. All three records are conference descriptions. There are no transcripts, slide decks, repositories, peer-reviewed papers, raw datasets, or independent replications in the supplied material. Tool availability is asserted, but the records do not establish whether the code was released as planned, remains maintained, or reproduces the stated findings.

Several research questions remain open:

1. **How is causal structure inferred from terminal data?**  
   The red-team record does not explain whether causal graph edges come from timestamps, command inputs and outputs, operator annotations, entity reuse, or a learned model [record_id:2722]. The validity of “causal edge density” depends heavily on this construction.

2. **How representative are the 95 engagements?**  
   The environments, objectives, operators, industries, security controls, and engagement rules are unspecified [record_id:2722]. It is unclear whether the findings generalize to criminal intrusions, internal penetration tests, assumed-breach exercises, or automated adversary emulation.

3. **What exactly does the deterministic TTP engine optimize?**  
   The automation record does not identify the engine’s state model, action space, objective function, or safeguards [record_id:2056]. It is also unclear how determinism is balanced against adaptation when defenses change.

4. **How exploitable is client ID enumeration in practice?**  
   FHIRbug establishes an information-disclosure pattern, but the record does not quantify downstream attack value, false-positive risk, rate limits, tenant isolation, or the relationship between client ID discovery and subsequent OAuth compromise [record_id:2723].

5. **How was the cross-vendor sample selected?**  
   The healthcare study gives counts but limited sampling detail. The representativeness of the 14 implementations, 16 discovery stacks, and 100 endpoints cannot be fully assessed from the abstract [record_id:2723].

6. **Can the methods integrate?**  
   The records invite, but do not answer, whether deterministic TTP selection could use causal knowledge graphs derived from real red-team behavior [record_id:2056] [record_id:2722]. Such integration could produce more realistic automated adversaries, but it would also raise safety, validation, and overfitting concerns.

7. **What defensive changes follow from the findings?**  
   The records suggest monitoring failed lateral movement, tracking hostname-oriented discovery, improving OAuth error hygiene, and testing implementation consistency [record_id:2722] [record_id:2723]. They do not provide measured evidence that these changes improve detection or reduce exploitation.

There is also little direct evidence of disagreement across the records. They are complementary rather than contradictory. The principal uncertainty is not internal conflict but whether the claims withstand full methodological review and external replication.

## Coverage And Evidence Notes

All three expected records contribute materially to the topic.

The DEF CON 33 record is a coauthored conceptual talk by Bobby Kuzma and Michael Odell. It addresses probabilistic versus deterministic adversarial decision-making and announces tooling for deterministic TTP selection [record_id:2056]. Because the abstract lacks results, it should be used primarily as evidence of research direction rather than demonstrated system performance.

The BSidesLV red-team record is attributed solely to Kuzma and presents quantitative findings from 95 engagements. It is the principal evidence for his use of causal knowledge graphs, his critique of kill-chain assumptions, and the Ithildin toolkit [record_id:2722]. Its numerical specificity is useful, but the absence of underlying data and methodological detail limits independent evaluation.

The BSidesLV healthcare record is also attributed solely to Kuzma. It provides the richest account of a reproducible testing workflow, cross-vendor comparison, product-level inference, coordinated disclosure, and the FHIRbug toolkit [record_id:2723]. It includes both positive and negative comparison cases and acknowledges a corrected technical attribution, which strengthens its methodological credibility. Some details remain intentionally withheld or aggregated because of active disclosure timelines.

Taken together, the records support a coherent portrait of Kuzma’s contributions: empirical interrogation of accepted security models, causal analysis of how knowledge enables action, careful comparison across systems, attention to error and failure artifacts, and release of tools intended to make the work repeatable. The evidence is promising but preliminary because it derives entirely from talk abstracts rather than full research artifacts.
