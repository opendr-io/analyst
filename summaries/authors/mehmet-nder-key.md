# Topic: Author: Mehmet Önder Key

## Meta-Summary

The three records attributed to Mehmet Önder Key span biometric authentication for constrained IoT devices, security testing of abandoned enterprise webhooks, and emulation of identity-first attacks against identity providers and cloud services. Despite their different technical subjects, they share a concern with **trust relationships that conventional security controls handle poorly**: static biometrics can be reproduced, integrations can outlive their owners, and authenticated sessions or identity workflows can be abused without deploying malware.

The work is strongly practice-oriented. One record proposes EEG signals as a lightweight, dynamic authentication factor for IoT systems [record_id:1932]. The other two outline reproducible offensive-security and validation workflows: a bug bounty methodology for finding “shadow webhooks” [record_id:3014], and ATT&CK-aligned purple-team playbooks for emulating identity-provider compromise [record_id:3017]. Collectively, the records suggest a trajectory from an experimental authentication concept toward enterprise-focused methods for discovering, demonstrating, and measuring security failures.

## Research Landscape

The corpus consists of three conference-talk descriptions associated with DEF CON events. The 2025 record is coauthored with Temel Demir and Dr. Ahmet Furkan Aydogan, while the two 2026 records are coauthored with Samet Can Tasci. Consequently, the evidence supports attribution of these topics to collaborative work involving Mehmet Önder Key, but it does not isolate his individual contribution to each project.

The records occupy three related areas:

- **Authentication and biological identity:** EEG-based user authentication intended for IoT constraints [record_id:1932].
- **Enterprise application and integration security:** discovery and safe validation of abandoned or weakly controlled webhooks across SaaS environments [record_id:3014].
- **Identity-provider and cloud security validation:** controlled emulation of identity-first adversaries across directory, SaaS, and cloud control planes [record_id:3017].

The source material is dominated by presentation abstracts rather than papers, datasets, implementation repositories, or post-event technical transcripts. These abstracts provide clear problem statements and intended methodologies, especially for the two 2026 talks, but little empirical evidence about performance, deployment experience, vulnerability prevalence, or detection outcomes. The research landscape is therefore best understood as a set of proposed techniques and conference demonstrations, not a body of independently validated results.

## Major Themes And Trends

### Trust should be dynamic rather than assumed

All three records challenge security systems that continue trusting an artifact, connection, or identity after its validity has become uncertain. In the EEG work, fingerprints and facial scans are characterized as static biometric mechanisms that are increasingly inadequate against deepfake-style attacks. The proposed alternative is to authenticate through changing electrical activity generated by the user’s brain [record_id:1932].

The webhook work applies the same broad concern to machine-to-machine trust. Enterprise applications may continue sending trusted events to endpoints after a service, repository, domain, cloud function, or responsible owner has disappeared. A previously legitimate integration can therefore become a data-leakage channel, forgery surface, or takeover path [record_id:3014].

The identity-first emulation talk similarly treats an authenticated identity or session as an insufficient basis for lasting trust. Stale sessions, weak conditional-access rules, device code flows, helpdesk processes, SaaS integrations, and cloud role mappings can all become routes for an attacker to pivot through otherwise trusted systems [record_id:3017].

Across the corpus, the recurring lesson is that security state decays. Biological factors may become reproducible, integration ownership may lapse, and identity controls may drift. Effective defenses must continuously verify current ownership, context, freshness, and integrity.

### Identity is treated as both a defensive primitive and an attack surface

Two records address identity directly, but from opposite directions. “No Brain No Gain” seeks a stronger basis for authentication by grounding it in dynamic EEG signals rather than stored static biometric templates [record_id:1932]. “Emulating the ‘Identity-First’ Threat Actor” examines how adversaries exploit identity infrastructure itself, including IdP configurations, sessions, device flows, and cloud role mappings [record_id:3017].

This contrast is useful. The former asks how a person can be authenticated using a biological signal that is difficult to replicate; the latter asks how attackers can still compromise access by abusing the systems, workflows, and delegated privileges surrounding authentication. Taken together, the records imply that improved authentication factors alone do not resolve broader identity-security problems. Session management, federation, support processes, SaaS access, and cloud authorization remain distinct attack surfaces.

### Forgotten and drifting configurations are a central enterprise risk

Configuration and ownership drift are explicit in the two enterprise-focused talks. The shadow-webhook methodology targets event listeners that remain configured after their destination or owner is gone [record_id:3014]. The identity-emulation framework targets “IdP drift,” weak conditional access, stale sessions, and problematic mappings between enterprise identities and cloud roles [record_id:3017].

These records move away from a narrow focus on software memory corruption or malware payloads. Instead, they emphasize security failures that arise from the accumulation of legitimate features and integrations over time. The dangerous condition may not be a newly introduced bug; it may be an old, trusted relationship that no longer has a valid operational basis.

### Safe, reproducible proof is prioritized over uncontrolled exploitation

The 2026 talks repeatedly foreground safe testing. The webhook presentation proposes demonstrating impact without collecting actual third-party data, using a controlled lab and synthetic project events or incident notifications [record_id:3014]. The identity-first framework likewise aims to simulate compromise and SaaS access without stealing real credentials [record_id:3017].

This approach serves both ethical and operational goals. It allows bug bounty hunters to produce triageable evidence without unnecessary exposure of sensitive data, while allowing defenders to test identity detections without creating a real compromise. Reproducibility is also central: webhook findings are structured around concrete evidence fields, and identity playbooks are intended to support repeated scoring and measurable purple-team validation [record_id:3014] [record_id:3017].

### The records show a shift toward enterprise attack-path validation

The 2025 EEG record is primarily a conceptual proposal for a new authentication layer under IoT constraints [record_id:1932]. The two 2026 records are more procedural and enterprise-focused. They describe inventories, risk classification, controlled demonstrations, telemetry expectations, detection hypotheses, and remediation controls [record_id:3014] [record_id:3017].

This may indicate a shift in emphasis from designing a novel security primitive to operationalizing security research as repeatable assessment and validation workflows. Because the corpus contains only three records, however, this should be treated as a tentative trend rather than a firm chronology of the author’s broader work.

## Methods, Tools, And Approaches Discussed

The EEG-authentication proposal uses the brain’s electrical activity as a dynamic biometric signal. Its stated design goals are to avoid storing sensitive biometric data, resist replication, and operate without heavy computation, making it suitable for IoT devices with limited processing capacity [record_id:1932]. The abstract does not specify signal acquisition hardware, preprocessing, feature extraction, enrollment, matching algorithms, liveness checks, or acceptable error rates. The distinctive contribution is therefore the proposed trust model—authentication based on changing EEG patterns—rather than a fully documented implementation in the available record.

The shadow-webhook methodology begins with **inventorying webhook surfaces** across enterprise tools such as Slack, Microsoft Teams, Jira, GitHub, CI/CD systems, monitoring platforms, and other SaaS integrations. It then classifies destination risk, fingerprints abandoned endpoints, and looks for dangling domains or retired cloud functions. The testing phase examines signature validation and replay behavior while attempting to avoid the collection of real third-party content [record_id:3014].

Its controlled demonstration models workflows across source control, ticketing, chat, and CI/CD. One scenario routes events to a retired destination; another uses a receiver without strong signature validation. Synthetic events are used to show both misdelivery and forged-event acceptance [record_id:3014]. The reporting workflow is also formalized: a triageable finding should identify the affected asset, trusted event source, destination ownership status, payload sensitivity, validation weakness, and resulting business impact. Defensive controls include webhook inventories, owner mapping, event signing, secret rotation, endpoint expiration, and domain lifecycle monitoring [record_id:3014].

The identity-first emulation framework uses automated, ATT&CK-aligned playbooks across Active Directory, Entra ID, Okta-like workflows, SaaS systems, and cloud control planes. It simulates identity compromise, IdP pivoting, stale-session or session abuse, and subsequent SaaS access without taking real credentials [record_id:3017]. The approach is explicitly purple-team oriented: each scenario should produce expected telemetry, test detection hypotheses, reveal failure points, and support repeatable scoring. This makes the framework not only an offensive emulation tool but also a measurement mechanism for assessing identity detections and control effectiveness [record_id:3017].

Although the records do not describe a single shared toolchain, they reveal a consistent methodological pattern:

1. Identify a trust relationship or authentication assumption.
2. Model how that assumption can fail or drift.
3. Reproduce the failure in a controlled environment.
4. Generate evidence that defenders or bounty triagers can evaluate.
5. Translate the finding into lifecycle controls, detection logic, or repeatable scoring.

## Notable Talks, Records, And Evidence

“No Brain No Gain” is the corpus’s most conceptually unusual record. It frames EEG activity as a dynamic, difficult-to-copy biometric and positions it as a potential answer to both deepfake-enabled impersonation and the computational constraints of IoT devices [record_id:1932]. Its importance lies in connecting human biological signals with lightweight device authentication. However, the abstract offers assertions about resilience, privacy, and computational efficiency without quantitative support, so it is strongest as evidence of a proposed research direction rather than a validated security architecture.

“Shadow Webhooks: Hunting for Dangling Event Listeners in Enterprise Workspaces” is the most detailed vulnerability-research record [record_id:3014]. It defines a recognizable vulnerability class—trusted event connections lacking a clear owner, valid destination, or reliable validation model—and converts that class into a practical bug bounty process. Its contribution is not merely observing that old webhooks are dangerous. It combines asset discovery, destination-lifecycle analysis, signature and replay testing, ethical impact demonstration, reporting criteria, and defensive remediation into a single workflow. Among the three records, it provides the clearest account of how a researcher would move from initial hypothesis to reproducible finding.

“Emulating the ‘Identity-First’ Threat Actor: Automated Playbooks for IdP Hijacking” is the strongest record on detection validation and adversary emulation [record_id:3017]. It reflects a modern intrusion model in which attackers exploit identity infrastructure and legitimate cloud access rather than relying primarily on endpoint malware. Its cross-platform scope—AD, Entra ID, Okta-like processes, SaaS, and cloud control planes—indicates an attempt to model identity attacks as connected attack paths. The emphasis on ATT&CK mapping, expected telemetry, detection hypotheses, and scoring makes it particularly relevant to purple teams and security operations functions.

The evidence is strongest where the records describe intended workflows, test environments, and reporting criteria. It is weaker where they imply effectiveness. None of the records reports measured false-positive or false-negative rates, numbers of real-world findings, detection coverage improvements, comparative benchmarks, or independently replicated outcomes.

## Gaps, Limits, And Open Questions

The most important limitation is source depth. All three records are short conference descriptions. They do not include complete presentation content, code, datasets, diagrams, experiments, audience questions, or post-talk evaluations. Downstream researchers should distinguish between what the talks propose to demonstrate and what has been empirically established.

For EEG authentication, major unanswered questions include:

- How stable are EEG-derived identity patterns across time, fatigue, stress, illness, medication, or different sensor placements?
- What are the false-acceptance and false-rejection rates?
- What hardware is required, and is that hardware realistic for everyday IoT use?
- How are enrollment and recovery handled?
- If sensitive data is not stored, what representation or verifier enables later authentication?
- Can an attacker replay recorded EEG signals or spoof the sensor interface?
- Does signal processing truly impose less computational or energy cost than established cryptographic authentication methods?

The record presents EEG as dynamic, lightweight, and difficult to replicate, but it does not provide evidence sufficient to compare it with passkeys, hardware-backed keys, behavioral biometrics, or conventional multimodal authentication [record_id:1932].

For shadow webhooks, the abstract does not establish how prevalent dangling listeners are in production environments or how often they lead to exploitable takeover rather than low-impact hygiene findings. It also leaves open how researchers can enumerate enterprise webhook surfaces when they lack administrative visibility, how they should navigate authorization boundaries in bug bounty programs, and how destination ownership can be proven when domains or cloud resources have complex histories [record_id:3014]. Additional research could distinguish between data exfiltration, event forgery, secret exposure, replay, and infrastructure takeover as separate severity classes.

For identity-first emulation, the available description does not enumerate the playbooks, telemetry sources, scoring formula, cleanup procedures, or platform-specific safeguards. It is unclear how faithfully “Okta-like workflows” model a real vendor implementation or how the framework handles differences in logging, licensing, token behavior, and conditional-access semantics. The record also does not explain how emulations distinguish malicious identity behavior from legitimate administrative activity—a central detection-engineering challenge [record_id:3017].

Across the corpus, there is no direct discussion of how the three areas connect architecturally. For example, the records do not ask whether EEG authentication would meaningfully mitigate IdP session abuse, webhook takeover, helpdesk exploitation, or delegated SaaS access. Stronger initial authentication may have little effect after a valid session or integration token has been compromised. This is an important open question raised by reading the records together rather than a claim made by any single record.

Finally, authorship is collaborative throughout. The records do not specify which concepts, implementations, demonstrations, or presentation sections were contributed specifically by Mehmet Önder Key. Conclusions about his individual research specialization should therefore remain cautious.

## Coverage And Evidence Notes

All three expected records are substantively relevant and have been included in the synthesis.

The DEF CON 33 talk “No Brain No Gain,” coauthored by Mehmet Önder Key, Temel Demir, and Dr. Ahmet Furkan Aydogan, supplies the corpus’s IoT and biometric-authentication component. Its raw text supports claims about the perceived limits of static biometrics and the proposed use of EEG signals as a lightweight, privacy-conscious authentication layer, but not claims of measured effectiveness [record_id:1932].

The DEF CON 34 Bug Bounty Village talk “Shadow Webhooks,” coauthored by Samet Can Tasci and Mehmet Önder Key, is the most methodologically detailed record. It supports the discussion of abandoned integrations, dangling destinations, weak signatures, replay testing, synthetic-event demonstrations, bug bounty reporting requirements, and webhook lifecycle defenses [record_id:3014].

The DEF CON 34 Adversary Village talk on emulating identity-first threat actors, also coauthored by Samet Can Tasci and Mehmet Önder Key, supports the discussion of identity-provider drift, device code flows, stale sessions, conditional access, helpdesk processes, SaaS integrations, cloud role mappings, ATT&CK-aligned playbooks, and purple-team scoring [record_id:3017].

No record is merely logistical or wholly peripheral. Nevertheless, the material should be treated as **proposal-level conference evidence**. The records establish the subjects, stated motivations, and planned methods of the talks; they do not independently verify technical efficacy, deployment readiness, or real-world prevalence.
