# Topic: Governance, risk, and compliance

## Meta-Summary

The records portray governance, risk, and compliance (GRC) as moving away from periodic attestations and generic control checklists toward continuous, evidence-based assurance tied to business, safety, and societal outcomes. Across vulnerability management, elections, critical infrastructure, AI, privacy, and third-party risk, a recurring argument is that formal compliance can coexist with serious insecurity. Better programs validate exploitability, measure actual behavior, test operational continuity, identify ownership, and produce evidence that decision-makers can understand and act upon [record_id:64] [record_id:94] [record_id:1854] [record_id:2533] [record_id:2597].

AI governance is the fastest-growing portion of the landscape. The records progress from fairness constraints and bias bounties to lifecycle red teaming, enterprise governance committees, agent identity, intent monitoring, behavioral audits, physical-AI safety, and the governance of autonomous agency itself [record_id:180] [record_id:242] [record_id:133] [record_id:2337] [record_id:2348] [record_id:2597] [record_id:2621]. The shared concern is that frameworks designed for deterministic software do not fully address adaptive systems that form plans, mediate decisions, manipulate users, or act in the physical world.

The collection also broadens cyber risk beyond individual assets. It examines vendor and technology concentration, fragile digital and physical supply chains, healthcare and election safety, water and food continuity, undersea cables, workforce infiltration, unsupported software, and the possibility that cyber incidents can destabilize firms, communities, or financial systems [record_id:66] [record_id:100] [record_id:2451] [record_id:2569] [record_id:2966]. Governance is therefore treated not just as an enterprise function but as a coordination problem spanning governments, vendors, operators, researchers, insurers, communities, and the public.

## Research Landscape

The collection is dominated by conference abstracts and session summaries from Black Hat, DEF CON and its villages, BSides Las Vegas, CAMLIS, Prompt\|\|GTFO, and [un]prompted. Most records describe proposed talks rather than completed peer-reviewed studies. Consequently, the corpus is rich in practitioner methods, frameworks, case studies, and policy arguments, but uneven in methodological detail and independently verifiable results.

Several source clusters have distinct emphases:

- **Black Hat policy and main-stage sessions** emphasize systemic risk, national resilience, regulation, public-private coordination, vulnerability prioritization, and increasingly agentic-AI governance. Examples include internet-scale concentration mapping, continuous testing for the Defense Industrial Base, CISA’s operational role, and debates over private offensive cyber activity [record_id:66] [record_id:94] [record_id:107] [record_id:2682].
- **DEF CON Voting Village** supplies one of the strongest coherent sub-corpora on assurance. It distinguishes trustworthy election evidence from ritualized auditing, examines unreadable barcode records and insider access, and connects technical research to legal protections and regulatory reform [record_id:1854] [record_id:1859] [record_id:1861] [record_id:1866] [record_id:1867].
- **BSidesLV and I Am The Cavalry sessions** emphasize practical program building, stakeholder communication, tabletop exercises, community resilience, and safety-critical sectors such as water, healthcare, food, energy, and aviation [record_id:2402] [record_id:2445] [record_id:2434] [record_id:2504] [record_id:3043].
- **CAMLIS records** contribute more research-oriented material on explainable vulnerability prediction, incomplete CVE records, model constraints, and lifecycle AI red teaming [record_id:181] [record_id:182] [record_id:242] [record_id:133].
- **Prompt\|\|GTFO and [un]prompted** show practitioners using LLMs directly for audit evidence collection, regulatory taxonomy generation, policy drafting, vendor mapping, enterprise AI discovery, and security guidance [record_id:2191] [record_id:2198] [record_id:2220] [record_id:2239] [record_id:2323]. These demonstrations also openly expose accuracy, privacy, integration, and access-control weaknesses.
- **2026 Black Hat, BSides, and DEF CON material** shifts sharply toward governing agency, AI-enabled decision systems, dual-use controls, AI infrastructure, and the pace mismatch between machine-scale exploitation and human-scale law or remediation [record_id:2597] [record_id:2621] [record_id:2891] [record_id:3093].

Overall, the research area is heterogeneous but connected by a common problem: how to turn abstract obligations and risk labels into operational decisions, reliable evidence, and durable institutional behavior.

## Major Themes And Trends

### From checkbox compliance to proof of effectiveness

The strongest cross-cutting theme is skepticism toward controls that demonstrate activity without demonstrating reduced risk. Phishing simulations may produce attractive click-rate dashboards while measuring lure quality more than durable behavior change; an eight-month study involving more than 20,000 employees reports that conventional training did not “move the needle” [record_id:91]. Later work makes the offensive implication explicit: standardized awareness can condition a predictable “Human EDR” that attackers learn to bypass [record_id:3003].

The same criticism appears in technical assurance. Continuous autonomous penetration testing is presented as a way to move Defense Industrial Base assurance from promises and periodic assessments to proof of exploitability, remediation, and revalidation [record_id:94]. Physical penetration testing and red teaming are likewise framed as incomplete unless findings are converted into safe, prioritized, financially intelligible upgrades [record_id:1914] [record_id:3061]. Security programs driven by controls without product context produce friction, rework, and “security theater” rather than useful risk reduction [record_id:2533].

Election records make this distinction especially clearly. Risk-limiting audits can bound the chance of certifying the wrong winner only when the underlying vote record is trustworthy; applying the procedure to an unreliable record is itself theater [record_id:1854]. Effective post-election audits must provide public evidence, not merely satisfy a procedural label [record_id:1862]. “Software independence” and evidence-based elections similarly shift trust from software behavior to independently checkable evidence [record_id:1864]. A later county pilot proposes open-source tools, distributed custody, public participation, and visible tamper evidence as mechanisms for producing proof that skeptical candidates and voters can inspect [record_id:2745].

This evidence-over-attestation trend also appears in medical devices. Static behavior analysis and “Software Bills of Behaviors” are proposed to answer whether firmware behaves safely for its clinical purpose—an assurance question that vulnerability scans, SBOMs, and CVSS scores cannot answer alone [record_id:3033]. Aviation work similarly argues that cybersecurity tools need complete, structured, traceable certification artifacts before they can be introduced into safety-critical airborne systems [record_id:3043].

### Risk prioritization is contested, contextual, and increasingly empirical

Vulnerability scoring is a major recurring concern. One record compares CVSS, EPSS, SSVC, KEV lists, vendor advisories, and practitioner judgment, questioning whether formal scores improve decisions or merely rationalize predetermined choices [record_id:64]. A more prescriptive session combines exploit likelihood, severity, contextual decision trees, and environment-specific exploitability through EPSS, CVSS, SSVC, and VEX [record_id:2523]. Another argues that teams focus too heavily on headline vulnerabilities and should first determine whether a risk meaningfully exists for the organization [record_id:2524].

CAMLIS contributions strengthen the empirical side. One framework combines topic modeling with threat and trend scores to predict both exploitation and vulnerabilities likely to escape community attention [record_id:181]. Another studies initially incomplete CVE entries and proposes a ticketing process for the dangerous period before records are updated [record_id:182]. Other records identify transparency problems in CVE participation and governance risks arising from funding pressures, parallel European databases, and ecosystem fragmentation [record_id:2549] [record_id:2571].

Several speakers argue that asset-level scoring is too narrow. Internet-scale scanning is proposed as a way to map concentration risk across vendors, technologies, sectors, and critical national functions in near real time [record_id:66]. “Risk surface management” starts from what the business stands to lose rather than the mere size of its attack surface, and proposes a Risk Operations Center as a financially oriented extension of continuous exposure management [record_id:104]. Cyber risk to financial stability may arise from operational weaknesses that amplify CVEs rather than from any one vulnerability [record_id:2569].

Evidence remains mixed. Some records offer sample sizes, datasets, or defined methods, while many announce frameworks without reporting validation. The most credible claims in this area are those tied to identified studies, operational datasets, or reproducible scanning; the weakest are broad assertions without methods or baselines.

### AI governance is shifting from model safety to lifecycle, system, and agency governance

Early records focus on model behavior. Shape constraints incorporate semantic knowledge, while rate constraints impose goals such as fairness or low model churn [record_id:242]. An algorithmic bias bounty session is potentially relevant to externalized fairness testing, but its session content is unavailable [record_id:180].

Later records reject model-only evaluation. AI red teaming should cover inception, design, data, development, deployment, maintenance, retirement, interfaces, supply chains, governance, users, disclosure, and post-deployment drift—not just jailbreak attempts [record_id:133]. STPA-Sec similarly analyzes hazards caused by inadequate feedback, interface ambiguity, and misaligned recommendations even when the AI has not technically “broken” [record_id:2458]. Physical AI makes this systems perspective urgent because sensor spoofing or embodied instruction manipulation can cause kinetic harm [record_id:2348].

Enterprise governance records advocate risk-based committees, cross-functional participation, lifecycle oversight, and structured paths to “yes, if” rather than blanket prohibition. A healthcare-services case discusses the successes and failures of a risk-based AI governance committee [record_id:2337]; Snowflake’s account emphasizes practical frameworks and collaboration [record_id:2338]; a publisher reports assessing 67 AI vendors in 15 months and red-teaming integrations involving student data and intellectual property [record_id:2741]. A tabletop workshop connects NIST AI RMF, the EU AI Act, lifecycle governance, and incident response [record_id:2387].

The 2026 records argue that even these frameworks may be insufficient for agents. A policy-gap analysis claims that NIST AI RMF, the EU AI Act, U.S. policy, and OT guidance can all be satisfied while intent-based threats remain uncontrolled; it proposes intent monitoring, agent identity standards, and behavioral audits [record_id:2597]. A related systems argument says the task is not merely to secure agents but to govern agency by reconsidering assumptions about control, authority, trust, decision-making, flow, and feedback [record_id:2621]. Accounts of AI discrimination and AI meeting assistants make the accountability gap concrete: autonomous systems may generate consequential records or decisions without adequate consent, logs, appeal mechanisms, or human intervention [record_id:2330] [record_id:2816].

The corpus also identifies cognitive governance risks. AI can encourage excessive cognitive offloading [record_id:97], generate personalized social engineering at very low cost [record_id:93], and participate in human-autonomy teams whose attack surface spans physical structure, cognition, networks, and policy [record_id:2779]. The concern extends to AI exploiting social, legal, financial, and political “code” faster than human institutions can patch it [record_id:2891].

### AI is also becoming a GRC automation layer—with material control risks

Several records demonstrate immediate operational value:

- A custom GPT retrieves Jira records for employee onboarding and termination audit evidence, reducing work across international audits [record_id:2191].
- A Claude Artifact generates compliance taxonomies, DLP entities, business intents, data classes, and regulatory mappings in JSON [record_id:2239].
- A workflow maps security vendors into the Cyber Defense Matrix with roughly 80% reported accuracy [record_id:2197].
- Gemini analyzes thousands of enterprise browser extensions and their privacy policies to identify AI use and data-processing locations [record_id:2220].
- ChatGPT and DeepSeek accelerate drafting of a multinational cyber-crisis policy paper and surface omitted considerations [record_id:2198].
- RAG and MCP are proposed to ground gap analysis, regulatory mapping, alert triage, and audit assistance in live policy, intelligence, and logs [record_id:2515].
- Centralized “Security Guidance as a Service” is intended to give multiple defensive agents consistent, evaluated guidance [record_id:2323].

However, the records repeatedly caution that speed is not reliability. Claude’s research assistance frequently selected irrelevant papers and required substantial manual correction [record_id:2185]. Vendor mapping achieved only approximate accuracy [record_id:2197]. Jira integration raised pagination, data-structure, GPT-to-GPT security, and data-sharing concerns [record_id:2191]. Enterprise adoption patterns show that deployment constraints, token budgets, procurement models, and culture may shape use more than written security policy [record_id:2339]. AI coding discussions likewise report disputed productivity gains and uncertainty around “vibe coding” [record_id:2201].

The practical governance implication is that AI-generated evidence or analysis needs provenance, validation, access scoping, human review, and explicit error handling. AI notetakers are an especially important example because summaries can become de facto official records subject to steering, discovery, consent obligations, and vendor failure [record_id:2330].

### Third-party, supply-chain, and concentration risk are central

Third-party risk appears both as a conventional vendor-onboarding problem and as a systemic dependency problem. A practical onboarding framework is proposed for evaluating vendors as cloud and AI adoption expands [record_id:2481], while SaaS platform assessments show why due diligence and independent testing matter when vendors process large volumes of enterprise and consumer data [record_id:2473]. Student privacy, healthcare software, food logistics, and AI supply chains demonstrate how external dependencies become governance issues [record_id:2005] [record_id:2434] [record_id:2451] [record_id:2531].

Concentration matters because failures can cascade across otherwise unrelated organizations. Internet-scale evidence points to overlooked industry-specific white-label SaaS providers as potential systemic chokepoints [record_id:66]. Food distribution, cold chains, energy transformers, subsea cables, and AI compute infrastructure all illustrate risk from concentrated or foreign-dependent resources [record_id:2451] [record_id:2504] [record_id:2847] [record_id:2966].

Unsupported software is treated as a market-governance failure, not merely poor patch management. “Forever-day” vulnerabilities persist in essential systems [record_id:2435], while proposed “graceful exit” legislation would require support-period disclosure, replacement obligations in some leasing arrangements, and clearer responsibility when vendors abandon products [record_id:2764]. Non-human identities add another supply-chain-like layer: service accounts, bots, tokens, and automation proliferate faster than traditional identity governance can control them [record_id:2572].

### Resilience is becoming a safety and continuity discipline

Many records reject the idea that preventing compromise is the only objective. Critical infrastructure must continue operating when communications, networks, vendors, or automated systems fail. CISA’s CI Fortify effort considers operating without phones or internet and under assumed breach [record_id:2841]. Hospital tabletop exercises continue after the “red team wins,” forcing participants to allocate care, handle unavailable medical devices, and question AI-generated remediation advice [record_id:3001]. Water, hospitals, food, and household preparedness are presented as interconnected continuity concerns [record_id:2494] [record_id:2845] [record_id:2846] [record_id:2847].

Cyber-Informed Engineering attempts to design out risk before systems enter operation rather than bolt controls onto deployed infrastructure [record_id:2436] [record_id:2437] [record_id:2833]. OT segmentation is similarly treated as change management under uptime, safety, vendor, maintenance, and ownership constraints rather than as a firewall-only exercise [record_id:3002]. Maritime incidents and the Titan disaster are used to show that weak oversight, legal gray zones, dismissed warnings, and untested designs can turn technological risk into loss of life [record_id:2068] [record_id:2092].

Healthcare records supply unusually consequential evidence. One session cites peer-reviewed claims of substantially higher in-hospital mortality during ransomware attacks and argues that existing coding and public-health systems fail to identify cyber-attributable deaths [record_id:3051]. The proposed response is “cyber-harm epidemiology”: adapting mortality coding, disaster certification, and international hazard statistics so patient harm becomes measurable and legally actionable.

### Governance is fundamentally organizational and human

Several records argue that risk ownership and institutional durability matter more than individual leaders or tools. A comparison between consecutive DNC security leaders asks which controls survived succession and which depended on one person; turnover created an accountability vacuum in which systems lost owners and risk became invisible [record_id:2665]. Program-building sessions therefore emphasize strategic roadmaps, leadership-relevant metrics, team design, stakeholder communication, and repeatable operational practices [record_id:2792] [record_id:2802] [record_id:2804].

Communication is itself treated as a control. Security practitioners need to translate vulnerabilities into business impact, quantify value, understand decision psychology, and become trusted advisors [record_id:2445]. Bug bounty programs depend on clear severity framing, respectful escalation, legal structure, and durable researcher relationships [record_id:1909] [record_id:1928]. Aviation vulnerability disclosure illustrates the consequences when institutions handle reporting inconsistently or defensively [record_id:2098].

The records also challenge simplistic human-risk categories. Role-based controls can over-monitor some populations and exempt powerful but risky groups; one study claims support from a 6,500-person survey, behavioral telemetry, and incident analysis, recommending action-based cohorts instead [record_id:2724]. Other talks discuss bias in controls and hiring [record_id:1880], selective protection decisions disguised as risk tolerance [record_id:2701], and algorithmic systems that impose unequal burdens without adequate logs or appeals [record_id:2816].

Workforce and community governance are part of the same landscape. Leadership panels address career progression, burnout, hiring, and board communication [record_id:96] [record_id:1874] [record_id:2760]. Civilian cyber corps, university clinics, volunteer programs, and mutual-aid work seek to extend defensive capacity to under-resourced communities [record_id:2001] [record_id:2422] [record_id:2762] [record_id:2835]. Labor organizing and researcher safe harbor address who bears the risks of security work [record_id:1861] [record_id:2499].

### Law, regulation, and public authority remain fragmented

The policy records describe persistent fragmentation across national, international, sectoral, and constitutional regimes. Black Hat policy meetups raise regulatory harmonization, deterrence, disruption, and U.S.–EU differences without claiming settled answers [record_id:108] [record_id:109] [record_id:2692]. Constitutional-law analysis argues that legal doctrine itself relies on flawed threat models, overvaluing some insider or outsider risks [record_id:103].

Election research demonstrates law’s dual role. DMCA exemptions enable good-faith voting-system research [record_id:1865], yet researchers in countries such as Nigeria may face criminalization, political retaliation, or denial of access, motivating broader safe-harbor models [record_id:1861]. New York litigation over unreadable ballot barcodes tests statutory voter-verification requirements that may have national implications [record_id:1866]. Border-search law similarly constrains what technical privacy precautions can realistically achieve [record_id:2421].

Offensive cyber policy remains unresolved. One record applies military campaign planning to ransomware and fraud disruption across technical, financial, diplomatic, and prosecutorial lines [record_id:2674]. Another examines proposed cyber letters of marque, private hack-back, constitutional authority, historical failures of privatized force, and AI acceleration [record_id:2682]. UK policy seeks an equilibrium between defensive security, state offensive capability, commercial intrusion tools, and responsible industry behavior [record_id:2695]. The collection does not converge on a preferred balance.

## Methods, Tools, And Approaches Discussed

A prominent methodological family is **continuous validation**. Continuous autonomous penetration testing, intelligence-led simulations, adversary emulation, and recurring tabletops are intended to replace infrequent, point-in-time assurance [record_id:94] [record_id:1939] [record_id:2129] [record_id:2402]. TotalTest proposes a metrics-driven attack-and-crisis simulation program, while roleplaying exercises use character roles, dice, MITRE ATT&CK, and dynamic injects to make incident training more engaging [record_id:2129] [record_id:2425]. AI-governance and medical-device tabletops extend this method to regulatory response, physical safety, and continuity of care [record_id:2387] [record_id:3001].

A second family is **systems-oriented threat and hazard analysis**. CARVER evaluates voting-system components through criticality, accessibility, recuperability, vulnerability, effect, and recognizability [record_id:1858]. STPA-Sec finds hazards in control structures and feedback loops [record_id:2458]. Cyber-Informed Engineering incorporates cyber risk into system design [record_id:2436] [record_id:2833]. TRIZ uses contradiction resolution and technology-evolution patterns to reason about supply chains, privacy, and infrastructure [record_id:2486]. The Cognitive Attack Taxonomy models threats across structural, cognitive, network, and policy layers [record_id:2779].

A third family is **data-driven mapping and prioritization**. Internet scanning maps vendor concentration [record_id:66]; passive OSINT assesses foundational controls across maritime organizations [record_id:2022]; threat-landscape workshops connect actors, tools, and assets to business priorities [record_id:2424]; and a national web audit escalates findings to Polish government response organizations [record_id:2867]. Explainable topic modeling predicts exploitation and attention gaps [record_id:181], while modern vulnerability workflows combine EPSS, CVSS, SSVC, VEX, and environment context [record_id:2523].

A fourth family is **control-framework mapping**. QRAMM evaluates post-quantum readiness through cryptographic visibility, data protection, implementation, and governance [record_id:2115]. FORTRESS maps physical-security TTPs to NIST, HIPAA, FedRAMP, ISO 27001, and PCI DSS controls [record_id:3035]. The Cyber Defense Matrix agent categorizes vendors [record_id:2197], and a capability-based AI-security method maps architecture-specific risks to existing, purchased, or custom controls [record_id:2347]. These methods are valuable for consistency but require empirical testing to avoid becoming the same checklist theater that other records criticize.

A fifth family is **governance automation with LLMs and workflow tools**. Demonstrated components include Custom GPTs, Jira APIs, Claude Artifacts, n8n, Google Sheets, Gemini, Obsidian, Zotero, RAG, and MCP [record_id:2185] [record_id:2191] [record_id:2197] [record_id:2220] [record_id:2239] [record_id:2515]. An AI avatar and AI-generated music illustrate less formal communication and awareness uses [record_id:2184] [record_id:2238], while ChatGPT-generated critique and metaphors support secure-by-design education [record_id:2195]. These are demonstrations rather than controlled evaluations.

Other practical approaches include event-driven password rotation and time-to-crack scoring instead of fixed expiry [record_id:2500]; cryptographic inventory and agility for post-quantum migration [record_id:1995] [record_id:1996] [record_id:2115]; action-based human-risk cohorts [record_id:2724]; federated privacy orchestration with service ownership and a central metadata catalog [record_id:2637]; and project-management methods adapted to security work [record_id:2802].

## Notable Talks, Records, And Evidence

Several records are especially representative or evidentially important:

1. **Internet-scale systemic risk mapping** offers a concrete alternative to hypothetical systemic-risk scenarios by using observed infrastructure relationships and concentration data [record_id:66]. Its significance is the shift from asset scoring to sectoral interdependency.
2. **The phishing-training study** reports a large real-world population and long observation period, making it one of the stronger empirical challenges to a common compliance control [record_id:91]. The abstract does not provide full statistical methods, so the result still requires review of the underlying study.
3. **Continuous autonomous testing in the DIB** connects exploit validation directly to assurance and compliance workflows [record_id:94]. Its operational backing is valuable, although the presentation involves a commercial platform and should be assessed for vendor bias.
4. **Risk-limiting audit and evidence-based election records** provide unusually precise distinctions among procedure, evidence, and trustworthiness [record_id:1854] [record_id:1862] [record_id:1864]. The wider Voting Village cluster adds system demonstrations, legal analysis, audit history, and insider-risk cases [record_id:1855] [record_id:1866] [record_id:1869].
5. **Lifecycle AI red teaming** is a foundational synthesis because it explicitly integrates models, data, users, governance, infrastructure, lifecycle stages, disclosure, and monitoring [record_id:133].
6. **The intent-gap and agency-governance talks** articulate the collection’s most advanced AI-governance thesis: compliance frameworks designed for instruction-following software may not control systems that adapt, plan, and act [record_id:2597] [record_id:2621].
7. **Roblox’s federated privacy infrastructure** is a concrete architecture for implementing regulatory rights across hundreds of distributed services while retaining data ownership at the service layer [record_id:2637].
8. **Security-culture succession at the DNC** treats program persistence across leadership change as measurable evidence of institutionalization [record_id:2665].
9. **Medical-device behavior analysis** reframes regulatory assurance around safety-critical behavior rather than vulnerability enumeration [record_id:3033].
10. **Cyber-harm epidemiology** identifies a governance failure in the inability to count deaths attributable to cyber disruption and proposes integration with existing health and disaster statistics [record_id:3051].
11. **Destroyed by Breach** challenges a widely repeated claim that 60% of small businesses fail after an attack and emphasizes incident handling over breach occurrence [record_id:2812]. Its larger contribution is highlighting the absence of reliable failure data.
12. **Dual-use AI governance at Anthropic** makes safety tradeoffs explicit through categories ranging from prohibited to benign use and proposes a Cyber Jailbreak Severity framework [record_id:3093]. It is important first-party evidence of provider policy, but not independent validation.

## Gaps, Limits, And Open Questions

### Evidence and reproducibility

Most records are abstracts, not full papers. Claims about effectiveness, accuracy, risk reduction, or cost savings often lack baselines, confidence intervals, comparison groups, and long-term outcomes. Even promising reports—80% vendor-mapping accuracy, rapid extension analysis, large phishing studies, or breach-failure datasets—need underlying methods and artifacts before they can support general conclusions [record_id:2197] [record_id:2220] [record_id:91] [record_id:2812].

The corpus is also heavily U.S.-centric. Nigeria, Taiwan, Latin America, the UK, Poland, and international cable governance provide valuable exceptions [record_id:1861] [record_id:2011] [record_id:2094] [record_id:2695] [record_id:2867] [record_id:2966], but comparative evidence on regulatory outcomes remains thin.

### Framework validation and interoperability

Numerous new models are proposed—Risk Operations Centers, QRAMM, TotalTest, FORTRESS, Civic AI, intent classification, capability-based AI security, AI threat taxonomies, and the DIE Triad [record_id:104] [record_id:2115] [record_id:2129] [record_id:2216] [record_id:2347] [record_id:2597] [record_id:2803] [record_id:2852]. The records rarely establish whether these frameworks outperform established alternatives, interoperate with one another, or remain useful across industries and organization sizes.

Open questions include:

- What evidence should prove that an AI governance committee reduces risk rather than delays deployment?
- How should agent identity, delegated authority, intent, memory, and behavioral drift be logged and audited?
- Can intent monitoring distinguish malicious planning from legitimate adaptation without unacceptable false positives?
- Which AI decisions require appeal, human review, or immutable provenance?
- How should governance cover multi-agent and human-AI teams rather than individual models?
- How can safety, security, privacy, and civil-rights obligations be reconciled when they recommend conflicting controls?

### Economics, incentives, and ownership

Many failures are attributed to incentives—vendor abandonment, insecure defaults, underfunded local infrastructure, fragmented ownership, compliance pressure, weak publishing incentives, or executive exemptions [record_id:2487] [record_id:2724] [record_id:2764] [record_id:2795]. Yet the collection offers limited quantitative evidence on who should bear remediation costs, whether insurance changes behavior, or how liability affects design [record_id:2574].

Third-party governance also remains underdeveloped relative to the scale of dependence. Questionnaires and onboarding reviews may not detect white-label concentration, fourth parties, model-provider dependencies, energy constraints, or vendor-controlled OT architectures. Research should test whether continuous technical observation, contractual controls, independent assessment, and concentration limits materially reduce losses.

### Societal outcomes and distributional risk

The records increasingly recognize that security decisions distribute protection unequally, but outcome measurement is sparse. Bias in control design, selective defense, algorithmic denial, patient mortality, surveillance, border searches, and harassment all raise questions about who receives protection and who bears residual risk [record_id:1880] [record_id:2701] [record_id:2705] [record_id:2816] [record_id:3051]. More work is needed on civil-rights impact assessment, meaningful consent, appeal processes, and public participation in technical governance.

### Pace mismatch

A final unresolved issue is institutional speed. AI-assisted attacks, autonomous agents, and automated exploitation may operate at machine speed, while patching, certification, legislation, litigation, and diplomatic coordination operate at human or bureaucratic speed [record_id:2891] [record_id:3043]. The records propose continuous testing, staged releases, behavioral monitoring, and stronger public-private coordination [record_id:2686], but do not establish how to maintain due process and safety when governance itself must accelerate.
