# Topic: Threat modeling

## Meta-Summary

The records portray threat modeling as a broad systems-design discipline rather than a narrow checklist for enumerating software vulnerabilities. Across AI agents, cloud identity, telecommunications, elections, medical devices, critical infrastructure, and human decision-making, the recurring question is not merely “what vulnerabilities exist?” but “what can this system, its operators, or its suppliers be induced or empowered to do?” The strongest records examine trust boundaries, architectural assumptions, authority, feedback loops, dependencies, and the ways individually modest weaknesses combine into consequential attack paths.

Three trends dominate. First, AI and agentic systems are forcing threat models to encompass perception, reasoning, memory, delegation, tool execution, and interactions among humans and autonomous agents. Second, cyber-physical and safety-critical work increasingly connects technical compromise to operational or human consequences rather than stopping at exploitability. Third, researchers are trying to make threat modeling more repeatable and scalable through taxonomies, structured frameworks, attack trees, simulations, source-code analysis, and AI-assisted workflows.

The evidence is uneven. Some talks report concrete vulnerabilities, experiments, or quantitative evaluations, including O-RAN testing, adversarial trading-policy attacks, and automated attack-tree generation [record_id:57] [record_id:203] [record_id:2664]. Many others are conference abstracts proposing methods or promising demonstrations; they establish the direction of work but not independently verified effectiveness. The collection contains little explicit discussion of classic STRIDE, misuse cases, or abuse-case notation. Instead, it emphasizes newer systems-oriented frameworks and domain-specific threat models.

## Research Landscape

Most records are conference talk or workshop descriptions from Black Hat, DEF CON, BSides Las Vegas, CAMLIS, and [un]prompted. The corpus is concentrated in 2025–2026, with only two 2021 CAMLIS records. Consequently, it captures current practitioner concerns and emerging frameworks more effectively than mature longitudinal evidence.

AI security is the largest cluster. These records address agentic applications, computer-using agents, multi-agent workflows, AI-enabled desktop applications, LLM threat elicitation, cognitive attacks, and automation of threat modeling itself. Several argue that jailbreak or prompt-injection testing is too model-centric and that deployed AI must be analyzed as a sociotechnical system spanning interfaces, data, tools, infrastructure, governance, users, and organizational processes [record_id:58] [record_id:127] [record_id:133] [record_id:1956]. Later records extend this systems view into human-autonomy teams, cognitive attack surfaces, and the governance of agency [record_id:2621] [record_id:2779] [record_id:2822].

Cyber-physical and operational technology form the second major cluster. EV charging, elevators, maritime navigation, water infrastructure, medical devices, and hospital continuity all appear. These records treat domain knowledge as essential to modeling impact: an elevator’s special modes, a mariner’s collision-avoidance rules, or a hospital’s need to continue care can determine whether a technical action matters [record_id:42] [record_id:1999] [record_id:2035] [record_id:3001]. Cyber-Informed Engineering workshops explicitly seek to design out risk before deployment [record_id:2436] [record_id:2437] [record_id:2833].

A third cluster concerns institutions, organizations, and people as threat-model components. Election-security talks distinguish cryptographic guarantees from endpoint and operational risk [record_id:1857] [record_id:1864]. Other records examine insider behavior, technology companies as structurally capable threat actors, constitutional-law assumptions, marginalized communities’ differing threat models, and attacks on human perception [record_id:201] [record_id:2000] [record_id:103] [record_id:2416] [record_id:2822]. This broadens threat modeling beyond technical architecture into authority, incentives, identity, governance, and harm.

The sources are practitioner-oriented rather than standards-oriented. MITRE ATT&CK, NIST AI RMF, OWASP guidance, and MITRE ATLAS are mentioned, but the corpus does not systematically compare established standards. It instead favors newly introduced mnemonics, taxonomies, workshops, and automated tools.

## Major Themes And Trends

### Threat modeling is shifting from component flaws to systemic behavior

A central theme is that a system can fail dangerously even when no component “breaks” in the conventional sense. STPA-Sec is presented as a way to expose hazards caused by misaligned recommendations, inadequate feedback, and ambiguous interfaces in military AI systems [record_id:2458]. Agentic-security work similarly argues that over-privilege and weak guardrails are symptoms of deeper assumptions about trust, authority, intent, control, decision-making, flow, and feedback [record_id:2621].

This framing also appears outside AI. ISO 15118 may mitigate some EV-charging risks while shifting or creating others, illustrating that security-oriented standards can introduce new attack vectors [record_id:42]. Legacy network infrastructure contains design assumptions that once held in cooperative environments but fail under modern adversarial conditions [record_id:2640]. O-RAN’s disaggregated, cloud-native architecture improves openness and interoperability while exposing new interfaces and inter-component dependencies [record_id:57]. The common lesson is that architectural change redistributes risk rather than simply reducing it.

Technology providers themselves may belong inside the threat model. One talk asks analysts to consider what cloud, rideshare, domestic robotics, and security companies are structurally able to do, including how insiders or external attackers could weaponize platform-level access [record_id:2000]. The constitutional-law keynote applies similar reasoning to institutions, arguing that legal doctrine trusts the wrong insiders, overreacts to outsiders, and adjusts poorly when circumstances change [record_id:103]. Both records challenge threat models that assume privileged institutions remain benign.

### Agentic AI collapses familiar trust boundaries

The AI records repeatedly emphasize that autonomy converts model-output errors into system actions. Computer-using agents combine screenshot perception, reasoning, memory, and user-like actions; their risks include visual deception, indirect prompt injection, chain-of-thought leakage, hidden privileged clicks, over-delegation, and execution paths that cross from browser content into operating-system behavior [record_id:127]. Multi-agent workflows add persistent memory, tool execution, software-supply-chain exposure, and inter-agent communication [record_id:1956].

Architectural controls are therefore preferred over treating guardrails as the primary defense. Proposed controls include explicit trust-flow modeling, input validation, privilege separation, reauthentication, policy-aware tool scaffolding, cryptographic attribution of actions, and restrictions around sensitive files or downloads [record_id:58] [record_id:127]. The claim is not that prompt injection can always be removed, but that its consequences can be constrained by architecture.

AI red teaming is also expanding from model testing to lifecycle and environmental analysis. One CAMLIS presentation criticizes the focus on eliciting harmful responses and calls for adversarial examination from inception and data acquisition through deployment, maintenance, migration, and retirement [record_id:133]. Block’s “Operation Pale Fire” offers a concise example of proactively red-teaming an open-source agent, although the abstract supplies no methodology or results [record_id:2362]. A broader literature-mapping proposal describes AI systems simultaneously as attack vectors, targets, and amplifiers, and hypothesizes that defense research is fragmented and poorly aligned with attack advances [record_id:2803].

The records also show AI being used to conduct threat modeling. Stripe describes agents for threat modeling and security-request routing, built with modular orchestrator/child designs, structured interfaces, targeted tools, validation, and offline and online evaluation [record_id:2324]. An MCP server is proposed to inject threat models, security requirements, and OWASP guidance into AI-assisted coding before generation, followed by post-generation verification [record_id:2373]. ThreatForest goes further by deriving structured attack trees from source repositories through six specialized agents and deterministic verifiers [record_id:2664]. These efforts recognize that AI-generated threat lists can sound plausible while lacking grounding or completeness.

### Safety-critical modeling must connect attack paths to consequences

The cyber-physical records repeatedly insist that technical compromise is only one part of the model. The Medical Device Kill Chain follows attack progression from debug access and firmware extraction through protocols, authentication, cloud trust, identity abuse, and patient-impacting integrity or alert failures [record_id:2649]. Newcomers analyzing an insulin pump explicitly confront a failure mode involving the human body, illustrating how safety consequences change research priorities and responsible-testing choices [record_id:2965].

Medical telemetry feeding LLM-based clinical systems creates a cross-domain trust problem. Crafted payloads may arrive as apparently authoritative sensor data and influence downstream summaries, alerts, or actuation. The proposed taxonomy spans analog spoofing, FHIR/HL7 free-text poisoning, MQTT injection, calibration and alarm hijacking, fragmented time series, and coordinated multi-device inputs [record_id:2971]. This is a particularly clear example of a trust boundary being missed because one subsystem treats another subsystem’s output as ground truth.

Hospital resilience modeling extends beyond preventing compromise. The “Lights Out, Last Call” tabletop begins after catastrophic downtime and asks how care continues amid imaging loss, firmware dependencies, patient rerouting, prioritization disputes, and potentially untrustworthy AI remediation advice [record_id:3001]. Likewise, election-security records challenge “magic bullet” thinking: end-to-end verifiability does not address malware or systems outside the cryptographic loop, while software independence and evidence-based elections seek recoverable evidence even when software is unreliable [record_id:1857] [record_id:1864].

Domain context also determines physical impact. Elevator security depends on lockouts, controller behavior, special operating modes, and hoistway access [record_id:1999]. Maritime analysis argues that manipulating a vessel into a port turn may have different consequences than a starboard turn because operational rules shape predictable behavior and legal responsibility [record_id:2035]. These records show why generic cyber taxonomies often need domain-specific consequence models.

### Threat modeling is becoming continuous and operational

Several records reject threat modeling or testing as a one-time artifact. The TotalTest framework criticizes point-in-time penetration tests and proposes continuous, intelligence-led attack and crisis simulation with metrics and organizational integration [record_id:2129]. The AI lifecycle approach similarly calls for ongoing monitoring, bidirectional feedback, and attention to behavioral drift [record_id:133]. Stripe’s agent workflow uses online and offline evaluation loops to improve quality over time [record_id:2324].

Automated source analysis is motivated partly by model staleness. ThreatForest argues that manually drawn diagrams and enumerated threats become obsolete as cloud-native systems change; its directed pipeline attempts to regenerate grounded trees from code [record_id:2664]. The vibe-coding record tries to place design-time security context directly in the generation loop rather than relying on a later AppSec gate [record_id:2373]. Together these records suggest a shift from episodic workshops toward “threat modeling as code,” continuous evaluation, and controls embedded in development pipelines.

However, the collection does not establish that automation can replace expert facilitation. ThreatForest’s own reported ATT&CK mapping accuracy is only 34.5% for embedding-only retrieval, despite stronger scores for threat statements, trees, and mitigations [record_id:2664]. Stripe notes that threat-model outputs are subjective and harder to evaluate than routing decisions [record_id:2324]. The stronger implication is augmentation with validation, not autonomous acceptance.

### Human cognition and social context are entering the modeled system

Several records expand attack surfaces to human perception and collective decision-making. The Cognitive Attack Taxonomy maps manipulation across physical structure, cognition, networks and trust, and formal policy in human, artificial, and organizational agents [record_id:2779]. “Reality Pentesting” proposes a five-layer topology for testing perception and decision-making, including reconnaissance, trust hierarchies, attack-chain construction, scoring, reporting, and consent boundaries [record_id:2822]. It explicitly identifies unresolved issues such as how to score cognitive findings, distinguish organic from implanted beliefs, and avoid awareness programs that merely cultivate distrust.

Threat modeling for individuals is presented as identity- and context-dependent. A community-defense talk proposes storytelling and harm-reduction approaches for explaining differing threats to activists, people seeking healthcare information, and marginalized communities [record_id:2416]. A general digital-resilience talk similarly warns that viral security advice often fails to address the risks people are most likely to face [record_id:2106]. These records argue against universalized models detached from users’ actual adversaries, capacities, and consequences.

Insider-threat work provides a more analytical version of the sociotechnical approach. It combines organizational, technical, psychological, and social factors and uses topic modeling over public narratives to construct adaptable incident models, then maps temporal, causal, and narrative clues from witness reports into graphs [record_id:201]. Although this is primarily retrospective incident analysis, it may feed future prevention by identifying motivations, targeted assets, bypassed controls, and organizational weaknesses.

## Methods, Tools, And Approaches Discussed

The collection presents a diverse toolkit rather than one dominant methodology.

**Structured elicitation and prioritization frameworks** include CARVER, whose dimensions—Criticality, Accessibility, Recuperability, Vulnerability, Effect, and Recognizability—are applied to voting-system components [record_id:1858]. PHANTOM-B is described as an eight-threat mnemonic for rapidly eliciting deployment-specific LLM risks, positioned as a practical alternative to broad catalogs and as a question-generating tool rather than a generic vulnerability list [record_id:2661]. TRIZ uses contradiction matrices and technology-evolution patterns to explore tradeoffs such as openness versus security or privacy versus functionality and to anticipate risks in supply chains, critical infrastructure, and data protection [record_id:2486]. The DIE Triad talk promises a paradigm that challenges conventional security assumptions, but its abstract does not define the triad or provide enough detail for evaluation [record_id:2852].

**Systems and hazard analysis** are represented by STPA-Sec, which focuses on unsafe control, feedback, interfaces, and system-level hazards in AI-enabled autonomy [record_id:2458]. Cyber-Informed Engineering uses fictional engineering projects derived from real cases, workbooks, and exercises to incorporate cyber-risk decisions before water or critical-infrastructure systems enter operation [record_id:2436] [record_id:2437] [record_id:2833]. The latter three descriptions are closely related, with the 2025 water-sector workshops duplicated across two sessions and the 2026 workshop broadening the framing to digital-risk mitigation.

**Attack-chain and taxonomy methods** appear across multiple domains. O-RAN research begins with architectural attack-surface mapping and a taxonomy of stack-level vectors before using dynamic tracing, static analysis, dependency discovery, and constraint-driven input generation [record_id:57]. MDKC supplies a seven-phase chain connecting hardware, firmware, protocols, authentication, cloud relationships, and clinical consequences [record_id:2649]. The medical-IoT prompt-injection taxonomy identifies seven routes by which malicious instructions could move through sensor and health-data infrastructure into LLM context [record_id:2971]. The Cognitive Attack Taxonomy and Reality Pentesting offer competing or complementary layered models for human and human-AI cognition [record_id:2779] [record_id:2822].

**Automated attack trees** are the corpus’s clearest direct connection to classic threat-model artifacts. ThreatForest scans code and passes findings through Scanner, Threat, Tree, TTP, Mitigation, and Report agents. Deterministic checks enforce structural constraints, while ATTACK-BERT retrieves only valid ATT&CK identifiers from a fixed catalog [record_id:2664]. The design’s significance lies less in using LLMs than in decomposing the task, grounding each stage in prior evidence, and applying non-LLM verification.

**Architecture-centered AI controls** include modular agent architectures, structured inputs and outputs, explicit validation, targeted tools, policy-as-code, fine-grained access control, and trust-flow modeling [record_id:58] [record_id:1956] [record_id:2324]. Computer-using-agent recommendations add DOM validation, cryptographic tagging, reauthentication, sensitive-path write controls, and virtualized downloads [record_id:127]. These controls aim to make invalid actions structurally difficult, even if model behavior remains probabilistic.

**Adversarial testing and simulation** range from lifecycle red teaming to tabletop exercises. TotalTest proposes continuous breach and crisis simulation [record_id:2129], while the hospital tabletop emphasizes post-compromise operational decisions [record_id:3001]. Threat-informed defense and adversary emulation are presented as ways to expose blind spots and strengthen defensive programs [record_id:1939]. OT threat assumptions are also updated in response to perceived convergence among state actors, ransomware operators, and hacktivists, though two records provide effectively the same talk description [record_id:2020] [record_id:2029].

**Data-driven modeling** appears in the insider-threat proof of concept, which uses topic modeling and narrative graph construction [record_id:201], and in adversarial deep-reinforcement-learning research, which defines a threat model and tests two active manipulation techniques plus passive policy imitation against benchmark and real-world trading agents [record_id:203].

## Notable Talks, Records, And Evidence

The O-RAN record provides some of the strongest empirical evidence in the corpus. It reports 26 previously unknown memory-corruption vulnerabilities, 20 CVEs, and operational effects ranging from degradation and crashes to system-wide failures. Its architecture-specific taxonomy is tied to automated testing, remote reachability, and concrete implementations rather than remaining conceptual [record_id:57].

ThreatForest is the most detailed automation proposal. It reports results across seven cloud applications, averaging 13 threats, 243 attack steps, and 77 ATT&CK techniques per application, with quality scores of 0.89 for threat statements, 0.84 for attack trees, and 0.92 for mitigations. Its transparent disclosure of weak baseline TTP-mapping accuracy is important: the abstract supports feasibility and structural rigor, but not uniformly reliable mapping [record_id:2664].

The adversarial trading-policy study is a compact but comparatively strong research record because it states a threat model, active and passive attack methods, and demonstrations against benchmark and real-world DQN agents [record_id:203]. The raw description does not provide effect sizes, experimental conditions, or reproducibility details, so conclusions remain bounded.

Among conceptual methods, STPA-Sec is notable for explicitly linking security to unsafe interactions and feedback rather than component failure [record_id:2458]. PHANTOM-B is notable for emphasizing rapid threat elicitation under deployment pressure and for distinguishing a system-specific facilitation tool from generic vulnerability lists [record_id:2661]. Both are highly relevant to pre-implementation analysis, but their abstracts do not provide validation designs or comparative outcomes.

The computer-using-agent taxonomy is one of the richest AI threat-model descriptions. Its perception–reasoning–action framing, seven risk categories, and concrete clickjacking, reasoning-leakage, and prompt-injection-to-code-execution cases make it useful for identifying boundaries that conventional browser or VM sandboxing may miss [record_id:127]. The architectural-control talk complements it by asserting that guardrails can create misplaced confidence and that trust-flow design is necessary for agentic applications [record_id:58].

The election records are representative counterexamples to control monoculture. E2E verifiability protects specific cryptographic properties but remains irrelevant to client malware and systems outside the protected loop [record_id:1857]. Software independence and evidence-based elections instead focus on retaining trustworthy evidence despite software failure [record_id:1864]. CARVER supplies a separate prioritization lens for assessing system components [record_id:1858].

The medical-device cluster is especially important for end-to-end modeling. MDKC connects low-level hardware access to patient consequences [record_id:2649]; the insulin-pump record highlights beginner-accessible, safety-conscious analysis [record_id:2965]; medical telemetry injection examines an emerging AI/IoT trust boundary [record_id:2971]; and the hospital tabletop tests resilience after controls fail [record_id:3001]. Together they cover design, attack progression, novel input manipulation, and operational recovery.

## Gaps, Limits, And Open Questions

The corpus contains almost no substantive treatment of classic STRIDE, data-flow-diagram practice, misuse-case notation, abuse-case notation, or formal attack-tree semantics. ThreatForest generates attack trees, but there is no comparison with expert-built trees, coverage measurement against known ground truth, or discussion of AND/OR decomposition quality beyond aggregate scores [record_id:2664]. Researchers seeking a history or direct comparison of established threat-modeling methodologies will need outside sources.

Many records are proposals or session descriptions rather than full studies. Claims about “complete” mitigation of natural-language injection, practical framework effectiveness, or new attack classes cannot be assessed from abstracts alone [record_id:58] [record_id:2649] [record_id:2971]. Future-event records may describe anticipated findings rather than completed peer-reviewed evidence, particularly the literature map whose results are explicitly framed as hypotheses to be presented later [record_id:2803].

Evaluation remains a major open problem. What constitutes a complete threat model? How should subjective outputs be scored? How much agreement exists among experts? Can automated models identify omitted assets and assumptions rather than only produce plausible text? Stripe explicitly encounters subjectivity in evaluating threat-model outputs [record_id:2324], while ThreatForest exposes mapping limitations despite structural validation [record_id:2664]. Cognitive testing lacks an accepted analogue to CVSS and raises consent, duty-of-care, and attribution issues [record_id:2822].

The frameworks are rarely compared. The records do not explain when analysts should choose STPA-Sec, CARVER, PHANTOM-B, CIE, an attack tree, a kill chain, or a cognitive topology, nor how these methods interoperate [record_id:1858] [record_id:2458] [record_id:2661]. Domain-specific methods may improve relevance but fragment shared vocabulary.

Threat-model maintenance is advocated but not operationally resolved. Source-driven regeneration and continuous simulations are promising, yet architecture may not be fully represented in repositories, and code analysis may miss business processes, human workarounds, vendor dependencies, or physical consequences [record_id:2129] [record_id:2664] [record_id:3001]. A key research question is how to maintain a model spanning code, infrastructure, policy, operators, suppliers, and observed incidents without overwhelming teams.

Finally, the corpus raises but does not settle governance questions. Who has authority to approve autonomous actions? How should an organization model a platform provider, insider, regulator, or government as a potential adversary? How should threat models account for different identities and asymmetric harms? Records on constitutional law, corporate capability, community security, and cognitive attacks expose these issues but offer limited common methodology [record_id:103] [record_id:2000] [record_id:2416] [record_id:2779].

## Coverage And Evidence Notes

All 43 records contribute to the landscape, but their directness and evidence strength vary.

The most directly threat-model-focused records introduce or apply recognizable frameworks: CARVER for voting systems [record_id:1858], STPA-Sec for AI hazards [record_id:2458], PHANTOM-B for LLM deployments [record_id:2661], ThreatForest for source-derived attack trees [record_id:2664], MDKC for medical devices [record_id:2649], and cognitive taxonomies or topologies for human-autonomy systems [record_id:2779] [record_id:2822]. Cloud-managed non-human identities are treated through provider-specific exploits and the question “what can go wrong?”, making that record a conventional architecture and identity threat model despite limited methodological detail [record_id:2552].

Several records are strong domain applications rather than general methods. These include ISO 15118’s risk-shifting effects [record_id:42], O-RAN architecture and testing [record_id:57], election verifiability and software independence [record_id:1857] [record_id:1864], elevator access design [record_id:1999], maritime operational consequences [record_id:2035], network-infrastructure trust assumptions [record_id:2640], insulin-pump analysis [record_id:2965], medical telemetry injection [record_id:2971], and hospital resilience exercises [record_id:3001].

The AI cluster spans architectural controls [record_id:58], computer-using-agent taxonomy [record_id:127], lifecycle red teaming [record_id:133], multi-agent security [record_id:1956], production security-workflow agents [record_id:2324], proactive red teaming of the goose agent [record_id:2362], security-context injection during AI coding [record_id:2373], and desktop applications that combine new AI risks with memory corruption, file parsing, protocol bugs, plugin exposure, fuzzing, and secure-SDLC practices [record_id:2428]. The “mental models” record contributes a higher-level systems critique of attempts to secure adaptive agents with controls based on static assumptions [record_id:2621]. The AI-landscape mapping record is broad and relevant but remains prospective in its stated findings [record_id:2803].

Critical-infrastructure training is represented by two effectively duplicate 2025 water-sector CIE workshop listings [record_id:2436] [record_id:2437] and a related 2026 digital-risk workshop [record_id:2833]. The OT threat-landscape records are also duplicate or near-duplicate versions of the same Mike Holcomb talk, both describing convergence among state, ransomware, and hacktivist capabilities [record_id:2020] [record_id:2029]. They support changing-adversary assumptions more than a specific modeling method.

Some records are adjacent to threat modeling rather than centered on pre-implementation design. The insider-threat NLP proof of concept models completed incidents from narratives and witness reports, primarily supporting investigation and organizational learning [record_id:201]. The adversarial trading study develops a threat model but focuses on experimentally manipulating deployed DRL policies [record_id:203]. The adversarial-mindset panel emphasizes red teaming, adversary emulation, and threat-informed defense [record_id:1939], while TotalTest emphasizes continuous attack and crisis simulation [record_id:2129]. These are valuable validation and feedback mechanisms for threat models, though not necessarily design-time elicitation methods.

The governance and human-context records broaden who and what must be modeled. Constitutional law is critiqued as trusting the wrong insiders and adapting poorly to change [record_id:103]. Technology companies are analyzed according to latent capability and possible misuse [record_id:2000]. Public-facing resilience advice is evaluated against realistic personal risk [record_id:2106], and community education emphasizes identity-specific models and harm-reduction storytelling [record_id:2416]. These records are conceptually important but provide less technical procedure.
