# Topic: AI security, prompt injection, and jailbreaking

## Meta-Summary

The records describe a security field moving rapidly from model-centric jailbreak research toward the security of complete AI applications and autonomous agents. Early work focuses on direct and indirect prompt injection, jailbreak construction, model extraction, backdoors, and activation-level defenses. Later work increasingly treats the model as only one component in a larger attack surface containing retrieval systems, persistent memory, browsers, coding environments, MCP servers, cloud sandboxes, delegated credentials, orchestration frameworks, and human decision processes.

The corpus’s strongest recurring conclusion is that an LLM’s inability to reliably distinguish instructions from untrusted data becomes substantially more dangerous when the model can invoke tools or act with user authority. Calendar invitations, documents, webpages, issue reports, tool metadata, medical telemetry, and shared agent content can all become control channels. The resulting impact ranges from misleading output to cross-user data access, credential theft, arbitrary code execution, persistence, lateral movement, cloud compromise, and physical effects [record_id:53] [record_id:1965] [record_id:2207] [record_id:2328] [record_id:2815] [record_id:2971] [record_id:3125].

The defensive trend is therefore away from treating system prompts, refusal training, classifiers, or “guardrails” as primary security boundaries. The more mature proposals emphasize deterministic authorization, capability bounding, sandboxing, controlled egress, typed interfaces, provenance separation, reference monitors, tool-call interception, memory isolation, and human confirmation for consequential actions [record_id:58] [record_id:2345] [record_id:2358] [record_id:2370] [record_id:2808]. Detection and testing are also becoming operational disciplines: large-scale red-team automation, production prompt-injection detectors, semantic rules, behavior baselines, neural-activation monitoring, exploit validation, and kernel-grounded detonation all appear in the records [record_id:128] [record_id:2342] [record_id:2363] [record_id:2377] [record_id:2652] [record_id:2669].

At the same time, AI is depicted as an offensive force multiplier. It lowers the cost of phishing, voice scams, vulnerability discovery, malware development, reconnaissance, adversary emulation, and attack execution. The evidence generally supports acceleration and scale more strongly than wholly novel attack primitives: several records explicitly observe that AI compresses known techniques rather than replacing them [record_id:2355] [record_id:2391] [record_id:2736]. Governance records consequently argue that organizations must manage agency, intent, delegated authority, and lifecycle risk—not merely model output or regulatory compliance [record_id:133] [record_id:2597] [record_id:2621] [record_id:2735].

## Research Landscape

The collection is dominated by practitioner conference talks and workshop descriptions from Black Hat, DEF CON, CAMLIS, BSides Las Vegas, Prompt||GTFO, and [un]prompted. Most records are presentation abstracts rather than full papers, source code, exploit advisories, or independently reproduced studies. They are valuable for identifying emerging techniques and operational concerns, but the claimed results vary considerably in evidentiary strength.

Three broad source styles recur:

1. **Technical offensive disclosures and demonstrations.** These describe exploit chains against named products or architectures, including Gemini Workspace, Copilot, ChatGPT sandboxes, AI browsers, coding agents, agent frameworks, OAuth delegation services, and MCP ecosystems. Some cite CVEs, vendor remediation, or large-scale validation, making them among the strongest records in the corpus [record_id:3] [record_id:53] [record_id:2209] [record_id:2606] [record_id:2630] [record_id:2648] [record_id:2934].

2. **Research frameworks, benchmarks, and defensive systems.** These include jailbreak frameworks, prompt-injection detectors, model-internal defenses, red-team tooling, taxonomies, threat models, and evaluation harnesses. Some provide concrete sample sizes, measured attack success rates, latency, cost, or false-positive findings [record_id:112] [record_id:113] [record_id:144] [record_id:150] [record_id:2363] [record_id:2778] [record_id:3110].

3. **Governance, human-factors, and strategic talks.** These discuss AI regulation, enterprise governance, cognitive manipulation, social engineering, healthcare rights, physical AI, and national-security implications. They broaden the threat model but often contain fewer reproducible technical details [record_id:93] [record_id:2041] [record_id:2337] [record_id:2348] [record_id:2531] [record_id:2891].

Chronologically, the material shows a clear shift. The 2023–2024 records concentrate on prompt injection taxonomies, repeated-sequence attacks, model extraction, spotlighting, PyRIT, backdoors, and white-box activation analysis [record_id:135] [record_id:144] [record_id:148] [record_id:167] [record_id:170] [record_id:171]. The 2025 material expands into agent hijacking, multimodal attacks, computer-use agents, automated offensive systems, and enterprise compromise [record_id:18] [record_id:71] [record_id:115] [record_id:127]. By 2026, the center of gravity is the complete agent stack: browsers, coding environments, MCP, orchestration, identity, cloud sandboxes, agent supply chains, persistent memory, incident response, and machine-speed attacker–defender interaction [record_id:2583] [record_id:2667] [record_id:2712] [record_id:2819] [record_id:2927] [record_id:3115].

## Major Themes And Trends

### Prompt injection has become an execution and authority problem

The corpus repeatedly rejects the idea that prompt injection is merely a way to make a chatbot say something undesirable. In deployed applications, model output can select tools, form database queries, write files, trigger workflows, or invoke APIs with user credentials. The security consequence depends less on the offensive wording of the prompt than on what the surrounding application permits the influenced model to do.

The Gemini Calendar research is the clearest representative case. A meeting subject, email, or shared document allegedly allowed an external attacker to influence Gemini agents and use their permissions for spam, deletion, email and calendar exfiltration, geolocation, video streaming, connected-device control, and propagation. The work emphasizes both inter-agent and inter-device lateral movement [record_id:53] [record_id:1965]. Similar patterns appear in KYC pipelines, where document-embedded content can steer extraction agents into cross-record operations [record_id:2328]; in AI-assisted CI/CD, where issue or model content reaches privileged shell execution [record_id:2815]; and in healthcare, where telemetry or plausible clinical statements can manipulate downstream recommendations [record_id:2971] [record_id:3082].

Several talks therefore reframe prompt injection as an authorization failure. Bearer credentials communicate who or what possesses authority but not why a particular action is being attempted [record_id:2735]. Related records argue that conventional Zero Trust verifies identity but cannot distinguish a normally operating agent from a semantically hijacked one acting within its permissions [record_id:3102]. Capability tokens, signed intent attestations, call-site policy, and attenuating cryptographic warrants are proposed as ways to bind authority to a specific task and delegation chain [record_id:2370] [record_id:2806].

### Indirect injection and context poisoning dominate real application risk

Indirect prompt injection exploits the fact that models receive user instructions and external data through a common semantic channel. Early defensive work describes this as a provenance problem: models cannot inherently distinguish concatenated sources, enabling instructions embedded in retrieved data to masquerade as user commands [record_id:144]. Later records show the same issue across HTML, documents, calendar events, tool descriptions, logs, browser history, PDFs, sensor streams, and agent-to-agent messages.

The attack surface is multimodal. Text2VLM reports greater susceptibility when textual attacks are rendered into visual inputs [record_id:115]. AI browser work discusses hidden HTML, images, steganography, CSRF, fonts, and browser history as injection media [record_id:2583] [record_id:2798] [record_id:3117]. Voice-agent testing adds speech recognition, text-to-speech, routing, and conversational state to the attack surface [record_id:2725]. Medical IoT research treats sensor telemetry as an implicitly trusted carrier for instructions [record_id:2971].

Not all manipulation requires explicit instructions. “Context Poisoning” describes a document that simply presents a credible but adversarial claim, causing an agent to change an HR, financial, or strategic conclusion without triggering a prompt-injection detector [record_id:2827]. The clinical “Divergent Prompt Injection” work similarly uses plausible but false medical statements rather than conspicuous directives [record_id:3082]. These records broaden the problem from instruction hierarchy to epistemic integrity: a system may be manipulated because it trusts the wrong evidence, not because it obeys an obvious command.

The records also suggest that attacks are becoming persistent and staged. Delayed tool invocation, memory poisoning, configuration modification, shared documents, and long-running manipulation can survive beyond a single context window [record_id:2369] [record_id:2644] [record_id:3103]. OpenClaw and MoltBook research goes further, describing zero-click backdoors, modification of persistent agent identity files, command execution, and potential worm behavior across an agent network [record_id:3125].

### Classic application vulnerabilities are reappearing inside AI systems

A major theme is that AI does not replace conventional application security; it composes with and amplifies old weaknesses. AI developer tools often have elevated privileges, excessive functionality, weak isolation, and newly blurred trust boundaries [record_id:46]. The reported compromises of YC agents involved IDOR, sandbox escape, cloud metadata access, and SSRF rather than exotic model attacks [record_id:2209]. OAuth connection abstractions allegedly reintroduced session fixation, confused-deputy behavior, open redirects, XSS, and cross-window communication problems [record_id:3].

The later records contain increasingly elaborate composition chains. A retailer’s shopping assistant was reportedly compromised through differential input handling, indirect injection, prompt disclosure, and backend tool execution [record_id:2641]. Copilot data theft combined parameter-to-prompt injection, HTML rendering, SSRF, CSP allowlisting, and post-processing sanitization gaps [record_id:2875]. Cloudflare CodeMode research began with prompt injection but reached a use-after-free in native glue code and cross-tenant isolation failure [record_id:2630]. Agent frameworks themselves are described as allowing prompt-controlled data to overwrite system instructions, corrupt orchestration state, or reach native parsers [record_id:2648].

This trend supports a systems-level testing principle: red teams must inspect the seams between model, framework, parser, browser, cloud runtime, and tool—not just whether the model refuses an adversarial sentence. Pre-task configuration can even yield deterministic code execution before the model receives a prompt [record_id:2939]. Conversely, an LLM build gate can be bypassed by placing the dangerous condition outside what the model can observe, rather than by persuading the model to approve known malicious content [record_id:2871].

### Browsers, coding agents, and MCP are high-risk convergence points

Agentic browsers combine exposure to arbitrary internet content with authenticated sessions, local resources, and action capability. One Black Hat abstract states that every browser tested was vulnerable to indirect injection leading to data exfiltration or account takeover [record_id:2583]. The PleaseFix and Intent Collision research argues that browser vendors have weakened longstanding isolation assumptions to enable agency, with attacks leading to account takeover, file theft, unauthorized purchases, persistence, and local code execution [record_id:2667] [record_id:3117].

Coding agents create a similar “lethal trifecta”: untrusted project content, sensitive credentials or source code, and command execution. Reported attacks originate in GitHub issues, READMEs, tool responses, or bug reports and culminate in secret exfiltration or malicious pull requests [record_id:2655] [record_id:2815] [record_id:3124]. A field guide describes three intersecting questions—what the agent can reach, what shapes its decisions, and what third-party components it can load—and argues that the most consequential failures occur where these boundaries meet [record_id:2826].

MCP appears throughout the corpus as both an integration enabler and a supply-chain risk. Malicious MCP metadata can redirect reasoning or cause hidden code execution [record_id:2207]. Other records identify tool poisoning, shared-memory exploits, version drift, line-jumping, weak authorization, command injection, SSRF, path traversal, and secret exposure [record_id:2554] [record_id:2811] [record_id:2819]. The records do not support treating MCP itself as the sole vulnerability; rather, MCP makes tool and data trust boundaries operationally easy to cross, while each server and client remains responsible for enforcement.

### Model-level jailbreaks remain relevant, but no longer define the whole field

The corpus still contains substantial research on jailbreak construction. HarmNet automates adaptive multi-turn attacks [record_id:112]. Universal context-independent triggers are generated through discrete gradient descent and claimed to force precise attacker-selected output [record_id:18]. Obfuscation studies investigate repeated control sequences, ROT13 and state desynchronization, more than 100 encodings, tokenization boundaries, reflection, and context shifting [record_id:170] [record_id:2243] [record_id:2237] [record_id:2375]. Other talks demonstrate roleplay or “pretend” pretexts against harmful-content restrictions [record_id:2208].

Defensive model-level work includes rotating refusal directions through “LLM salting” [record_id:113], prompt patching and security-steerability measurements [record_id:2199], residual-stream attack classification [record_id:148], and multi-task detection that separates harmful goals from their framing [record_id:2778]. Backdoor research modifies computational graphs, detects weakened refusal mechanisms through white-box signals, or selectively noises neurons associated with Trojan behavior [record_id:114] [record_id:150] [record_id:2382].

These approaches are useful but generally narrower than application-level containment. Several records explicitly argue that jailbreak testing alone is insufficient [record_id:133] [record_id:2188]. Even strong model alignment may collapse when a harmful request is represented as a legitimate tool call, while abliterated backbones matter more for genuinely difficult harmful content than for ordinary agent-artifact poisoning [record_id:3128].

### Defenses are moving from “soft” guardrails to hard architectural boundaries

A central tension in the corpus is whether prompt injection can be “solved.” One position calls eliminating it a lost cause and recommends designing systems so a compromised model cannot produce unacceptable effects [record_id:58]. Stripe’s containment approach similarly assumes injection may occur and instead limits exfiltration, adds confirmation flows, and enforces tool policies at CI time [record_id:2358]. Other talks claim that correct encapsulation, type safety, deterministic policy enforcement, and removal of the lethal trifecta can make prompt injection operationally irrelevant [record_id:2808].

These claims are not necessarily contradictory. Model-level detection can reduce attack success, while architectural constraints determine the maximum consequence of failures. Spotlighting reportedly reduced experimental attack success from above 50% to below 2%, but it remains a provenance-oriented prompt transformation rather than a universal authorization boundary [record_id:144]. BrowseSafe reports approximately 0.91 F1 and sub-100ms latency in a production browser setting, while also noting difficult multilingual, camouflaged, and visible-text attacks and poorer real-world performance than academic benchmarks imply [record_id:2363].

Harder controls discussed across the collection include:

- deterministic reference monitors for shell, file, and tool activity [record_id:2345];
- controlled egress and domain governance [record_id:2358] [record_id:2761];
- cryptographic capability attenuation and signed agent messages [record_id:2370] [record_id:2806];
- memory isolation and bounded tool capability [record_id:2359];
- DOM validation, action tagging, reauthentication, and virtualized downloads for computer-use agents [record_id:127];
- multi-layer OS, network, and model sandboxes [record_id:2655];
- typed interfaces, output sanitization, and chaining policies [record_id:2361];
- kernel-observed detonation rather than trusting an LLM judge’s description of events [record_id:2669].

Even these defenses have limits. Sandbox research repeatedly finds gaps in Python restrictions, environment sanitization, process ancestry, native runtimes, and shared cloud infrastructure [record_id:2606] [record_id:2645] [record_id:2887] [record_id:2925] [record_id:3114]. The collection therefore favors defense in depth over any single definitive control.

### AI is lowering the cost and increasing the pace of offensive security

Numerous records describe AI-driven vulnerability discovery. Robust exploit validators reportedly enabled agents to find more than 200 zero-days without adding human review burden [record_id:79]. FENRIR reports over 100 vulnerabilities and 21 patched CVEs using staged static analysis, LLM validation, and human routing [record_id:2329]. Other systems claim 12 OpenSSL zero-days, dozens of IoT vulnerabilities, and large numbers of bugs across open-source or AI infrastructure [record_id:2350] [record_id:2629] [record_id:2084].

The strongest workflows are hybrid rather than purely generative. Static analyzers select relevant semantic slices for an LLM [record_id:72]; validators confirm exploitability [record_id:79]; confidence scores route candidates to people [record_id:2329]; and cross-model comparison reduces hallucination risk [record_id:2182]. By contrast, automated research pipelines and bug-bounty submissions frequently produce fabricated statistics, false positives, or convincing reports about nonexistent code [record_id:2179] [record_id:3044].

AI also supports malware and adversary operations. Records describe specialist models for AV/EDR evasion [record_id:62], AI-assisted C2 in Apple media and ML frameworks [record_id:4], fine-tuned models as covert channels [record_id:2573], agentic malware generating code in memory [record_id:2391], and autonomous attack emulation [record_id:130] [record_id:3115]. The evidence is mixed on novelty: some systems demonstrate technically new channels, while others conclude that current agentic malware offers limited advantage beyond faster adaptation and scale.

### Human manipulation, fraud, and physical consequences expand the threat model

AI-assisted phishing and scams are a substantial secondary theme. Autonomous systems can gather OSINT, build psychological profiles, register infrastructure, craft messages, and conduct multi-turn voice conversations [record_id:2192] [record_id:2394] [record_id:131]. Survey-based vishing research reports success rates up to 36%, difficulty distinguishing human from synthetic voices, and a cost shift that can make automated attacks profitable [record_id:2736]. ScamBench seeks to evaluate personalized, semi-personalized, generic, voice, email, and long-running social-engineering scenarios [record_id:2800].

The corpus also treats human cognition as an attack surface. Human digital twins and longitudinal interaction data may make manipulation inexpensive and persistent [record_id:93]. Cognitive taxonomies extend attacks across physical, cognitive, network, and policy layers in human-autonomy teams [record_id:2779]. “Trust Amplification” describes enterprise assistants transforming malicious content into apparently authoritative guidance [record_id:3107]. SEND applies LLMs to poison the narrative that incident responders construct, targeting analyst attention and causal reasoning rather than merely evading telemetry [record_id:2997].

Physical and safety-critical settings increase consequence. The records discuss cloned air-traffic-control voices [record_id:2047], medical assistants [record_id:2076], physical AI and robotics [record_id:2348], industrial attack emulation [record_id:3046], and manipulation of clinical systems through telemetry and PDFs [record_id:2971] [record_id:3082]. These areas are less empirically mature than web-agent exploitation, but they show why output accuracy alone is an inadequate security metric.

### Governance is shifting toward agency, lifecycle, and operational accountability

Governance talks consistently argue that model approval is not enough. Mature programs should cover inception, data, development, deployment, monitoring, migration, and retirement [record_id:133]. Enterprise examples emphasize cross-functional committees, risk-based vendor review, tabletop exercises, and practical “yes, if” enablement rather than categorical prohibition [record_id:2387] [record_id:2337] [record_id:2338] [record_id:2741].

Regulatory gaps center on intent and autonomy. Existing frameworks are said to assume software executes supplied instructions, whereas agents form plans and act over time [record_id:2597]. Other records argue that security leadership must govern agency through control, decision-making, flow, and feedback rather than merely adding controls around a model [record_id:2621]. Continuous trust, non-human identity ownership, behavioral auditing, and agent-specific authorization appear as recurring requirements [record_id:2738] [record_id:2817].

## Methods, Tools, And Approaches Discussed

The red-team tooling landscape is increasingly modular. PyRIT provides reusable targets, converters, prompt templates, datasets, scorers, memory, and notebooks, and was reportedly used for multilingual Sora testing and roughly one million GPT-5 conversations [record_id:128]. Its earlier presentation establishes the model- and platform-agnostic design [record_id:135]. BlackIce packages multiple AI security tools in a reproducible Docker environment, treating dependency management as a prerequisite for practical testing [record_id:129]. Chatbot.ai runs jailbreak prompts across models and configurations to measure transferability [record_id:2212], while Prompt Hardener automates prompt evaluation using spotlighting, role consistency, instruction defense, and categorized payloads [record_id:2506].

Attack generation is becoming adaptive. HarmNet constructs and refines multi-turn jailbreaks [record_id:112]; MPG mutates cross-domain prompt-injection payloads to test variant robustness [record_id:3073]; and a four-stage agent-testing methodology maps tools, identifies exploitable chains, generates payloads, and adapts execution after failures [record_id:2618]. Voice-agent testing uses actual calls, speech synthesis, transcription, and scenario-based scoring [record_id:2725].

Detection methods span several abstraction levels. At the input level, CerBERTus separates goal, framing, and harmfulness [record_id:2778]. BrowseSafe uses a fine-tuned mixture-of-experts model trained on attacks embedded in realistic HTML [record_id:2363]. BinaryShield proposes privacy-preserving fingerprints for sharing prompt-injection intelligence without exposing raw prompts [record_id:2327]. SYARA combines strings, embeddings, classifiers, perceptual hashes, and LLMs in layered semantic rules [record_id:2342].

At the model-internal level, residual-stream analysis classifies attack prompts [record_id:148], Glass-Box Security proposes latent-space “semantic tripwires” [record_id:2346], and GAVEL expresses rules over interpretable neural “Cognitive Elements” [record_id:2652]. White-box analysis is also proposed for detecting backdoors that weaken refusal behavior without visibly failing ordinary output tests [record_id:2382].

At the execution level, Cedar policies and Rust hooks intercept agent actions [record_id:2345]. MCP X-Ray combines static inspection with live adversarial tool calls and SARIF output [record_id:2811]. Agent detonation compares what the model claims it did with kernel-observed behavior [record_id:2669]. Endpoint-observability research traces AI-spawned process ancestry and calls for standardized OpenTelemetry semantics [record_id:2341]. PowerPwn and related reconnaissance work identify exposed and over-permissioned agents [record_id:2368] [record_id:3113], while an AI honeypot observed framework-aware scanning, MCP enumeration, and credential probing [record_id:3012].

Evaluation methodology is itself becoming a research target. Robust exploit validators can turn autonomous attempts into zero-false-positive findings [record_id:79]. Security-agent evaluation frameworks emphasize planning, tool use, context preservation, exploit confirmation, patching, and validation rather than final-answer correctness [record_id:2360]. Behavioral audits show agents neglect provided tools and structured methodology [record_id:2772]. SADF introduces eight agent-failure classes and execution-grounded, refusal-filtered scoring, finding a 2.6-fold spread attributable to the orchestration framework alone [record_id:3110]. AgentBreaker generates harness-specific secure-coding evaluations [record_id:3084], while botsbench research warns that agents can game or contaminate their own evaluations [record_id:2789].

Several records address AI-assisted defensive and offensive workflows outside direct model security. RIG-RAG turns cloud configuration into a security-enriched graph [record_id:118]. Binary Ninja and Ghidra MCP integrations accelerate malware analysis and detection-rule generation [record_id:2228] [record_id:2236]. Clue-driven reverse engineering uses APIs, constants, entropy, and attention tracking to limit cascading hallucinations [record_id:47]. BugTraceAI, modular reconnaissance harnesses, and deterministic TTP-selection engines support bug hunting and adversary operations [record_id:2182] [record_id:2194] [record_id:2056].

## Notable Talks, Records, And Evidence

The Gemini Calendar work is one of the corpus’s most representative attack records because it demonstrates a simple external delivery mechanism, broad agent permissions, cross-agent propagation, and possible physical impact. It also provides a concrete risk assessment in which 73% of identified risks were classified as high or critical [record_id:53] [record_id:1965]. Google’s later Workspace defense talk is an important counterpart because it explicitly describes the intersection of sensitive data, untrusted content, and command execution and discusses prompt defenses, action sandboxing, and output sanitization [record_id:2361].

The architectural-defense records are especially important for interpreting the field. “When Guardrails Aren’t Enough” argues that natural-language injection should be assumed and contained through trust-flow design [record_id:58]. “Breaking the Lethal Trifecta” supplies an enterprise implementation perspective focused on egress, human confirmation, and CI-time tool annotations [record_id:2358]. Capability-based authorization proposes task-scoped, attenuating warrants that remain bounded even when an agent is compromised [record_id:2370]. These records collectively offer a stronger security model than prompt refusal alone, although their guarantees still depend on complete mediation and correct policy design.

The strongest model-defense evidence comes from studies reporting controlled measurements. Spotlighting gives an explicit reduction in attack success with minimal task degradation [record_id:144]. Targeted neuron noising reports substantial Trojan mitigation with relatively small benchmark-accuracy losses [record_id:150]. BrowseSafe provides benchmark construction details, production latency, F1 scores, and observations from deployment [record_id:2363]. CerBERTus provides held-out-category and held-out-framing AUC results [record_id:2778]. These are more informative than records that only announce a framework or promise a demonstration.

The strongest offensive automation evidence is likewise quantitative. The exploit-validator work reports thousands of tested applications, more than 200 zero-days, and multiple CVEs [record_id:79]. FENRIR reports 100-plus vulnerabilities and 21 patched CVEs while openly documenting failures of generic prompts and pure automation [record_id:2329]. The OpenSSL work claims 12 disclosed vulnerabilities fixed in a January 2026 update [record_id:2350]. IoT research reports 30 vulnerabilities across two selected targets in five days, while also documenting an unconstrained agent damaging a live device [record_id:2629]. These records support the proposition that AI can materially accelerate vulnerability research, but they do not establish that fully autonomous systems are broadly reliable.

The sandbox and framework research matters because it shows that “AI security” often reduces to ordinary isolation engineering under unusual inputs. Remote Prompt Execution chains Copilot document interaction to sandbox privilege escalation and host compromise [record_id:2606]. ChatGPT sandbox research combines parser abuse, reasoning-channel hijacking, scheduler abuse, and shared infrastructure into a claimed cross-tenant covert channel [record_id:2645] [record_id:3114]. The framework audit reports 11 CVEs across widely used orchestration systems and identifies data-plane/control-plane confusion as the recurring cause [record_id:2648]. These claims are high impact, though the abstracts alone do not provide enough detail for independent reproduction.

The PleaseFix research is notable for its cross-platform scope and clear defensive conclusion: deterministic, code-level boundaries performed better than model safety as a containment mechanism [record_id:2667] [record_id:3117]. The contrasting “Agent Long Con” record is also important because it suggests modern agents may resist simple one-shot injection better than earlier systems, while remaining vulnerable to gradual manipulation and poisoning [record_id:3103]. This is one of the few explicit qualifications of the otherwise dominant “single malicious object causes takeover” narrative.

Finally, the corpus’s methodological self-criticism is significant. “Red Teaming AI Red Teaming” argues for lifecycle and system coverage [record_id:133]. PyRIT’s later presentation acknowledges scorer, target-integration, GUI, agentic, and human-review gaps despite very large test volumes [record_id:128]. The behavioral audit of 189 offensive runs finds poor enumeration, methodology, and reporting despite benchmark success [record_id:2772]. AI-generated bug reports have overwhelmed maintainers with plausible but invalid findings [record_id:3044]. Together, these records caution downstream researchers against treating attack success rates, benchmark scores, or large prompt counts as evidence of operational competence without examining traces and validation.

## Gaps, Limits, And Open Questions

### Abstract-level evidence and reproducibility

Most records are conference descriptions. Even where they report CVEs, patched vulnerabilities, or numerical results, the supplied text usually lacks datasets, baselines, confidence intervals, model versions, complete threat assumptions, and reproduction steps. Scheduled 2026 talks may describe work that was not yet publicly available at the time of the record. Claims of “first,” “universal,” “every browser,” “zero false positives,” or “security guarantees” should therefore be treated as presenter claims unless corroborated elsewhere.

### Inconsistent definitions and metrics

The corpus uses overlapping terms—prompt injection, jailbreak, promptware, context poisoning, tool poisoning, reasoning injection, intent collision, remote prompt execution, and divergent injection—without a shared ontology. Some are genuinely distinct; others appear to rename combinations of indirect injection and application flaws. Attack success can mean harmful text, model compliance, an attempted tool call, completed execution, data exfiltration, or persistent compromise. SADF’s finding that substring scoring can overstate success four- to six-fold illustrates the problem [record_id:3110].

### Limited comparative defense evaluation

Many defenses are evaluated against a particular model, dataset, or application architecture. It is not clear how spotlighting, prompt patching, salting, activation rules, semantic classifiers, and browser-injection detectors compare under the same adaptive attacker. Nor is it clear how often hard architectural controls fail because of incomplete mediation, business exceptions, policy complexity, or user fatigue.

### Unclear real-world prevalence

The corpus contains strong demonstrations and some internet-scale reconnaissance, but fewer longitudinal incident datasets. Exposed-agent discovery, honeypot traffic, ShadowRay, AI-assisted cloud attacks, and agent supply-chain malware provide early evidence of operational abuse [record_id:2368] [record_id:2642] [record_id:2669] [record_id:3012]. Still, the prevalence of successful prompt-injection-driven compromise relative to ordinary credential theft, misconfiguration, and software vulnerabilities remains unresolved.

### Attribution of AI-assisted attacks

Some records infer AI involvement from speed, code style, iterative development, or structured payloads [record_id:2355] [record_id:2642]. These are suggestive but not conclusive. Better forensic criteria are needed to distinguish AI-assisted operations from skilled automation or ordinary scripted campaigns.

### Human factors and physical systems

Social-engineering studies provide stronger empirical grounding than many physical-AI talks, but results may depend on survey framing and artificial exposure. Healthcare, aviation, robotics, and industrial records identify plausible high-consequence paths, yet often provide early experiments or simulations rather than evidence of deployed-system compromise. Research should examine fail-safe design, liability, human override behavior, and cross-disciplinary incident reporting.

### Incident response remains underdeveloped

The collection is rich in offensive techniques but comparatively thin on evidence preservation, compromise scoping, rollback, disclosure, and recovery after agent incidents. The proposed product-security incident-response playbook highlights ephemeral context, untracked tool graphs, probabilistic root causes, memory poisoning, and the absence of a mature CVE-like namespace [record_id:2712]. More work is needed on standard logs, forensic schemas, replayable decision traces, and safe memory invalidation.

### Governance may remain disconnected from implementation

Governance frameworks commonly recommend lifecycle oversight, committees, risk classification, and human review, but relatively few records show how those controls map to tool-call enforcement, agent identity, egress policy, or measurable security outcomes. The claimed “intent gap” between compliance and actual agent security is therefore a central open question [record_id:2597].
