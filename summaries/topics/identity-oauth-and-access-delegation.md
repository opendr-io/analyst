# Topic: Identity, OAuth, and access delegation

## Meta-Summary

The records portray identity as the connective tissue—and increasingly the primary attack surface—of enterprise, cloud, SaaS, mobile, physical-access, and agentic-AI systems. The central problem is no longer limited to stealing a password. Attackers can instead acquire or redirect OAuth grants, replay sessions, inherit machine privileges, manipulate authentication protocols, exploit recovery paths, compromise identity-management infrastructure, or induce an already-authorized agent or platform service to act on their behalf.

Several recurring conclusions emerge:

- **Authentication and authorization remain routinely conflated.** Applications accept a successfully authenticated identity without adequately checking tenant, audience, resource, role, intent, or ownership. This appears in Entra applications, APIs, OAuth/OIDC stacks, cloud service agents, and physical or biometric systems [record_id:20] [record_id:2018] [record_id:2723].
- **Delegation expands both capability and ambiguity.** OAuth token stores, managed identities, service agents, workload federation, SaaS connectors, and AI tools create chains in which a low-privileged caller can exercise a more privileged identity. Confused-deputy behavior and weak lifecycle controls recur across Azure, GCP, AWS, and integration platforms [record_id:3] [record_id:15] [record_id:2672] [record_id:2684].
- **Passwordless does not mean attackless.** Passkeys and biometrics improve important parts of authentication, but surrounding implementations, recovery methods, client APIs, synchronization, attestation, browser extensions, and alternate sign-in paths remain exploitable [record_id:45] [record_id:1992] [record_id:1997] [record_id:2590] [record_id:2945].
- **Sessions and delegated tokens are replacing credentials as the preferred target.** Reverse proxies, Browser-in-the-Middle systems, cookie theft, PRT extraction, OAuth consent phishing, device-code phishing, and infostealers let attackers bypass or inherit MFA rather than defeat it cryptographically [record_id:156] [record_id:1987] [record_id:2543] [record_id:2775] [record_id:2859] [record_id:2942].
- **Non-human identity is becoming a first-class security problem.** Service accounts, CI/CD identities, workload tokens, OAuth applications, cloud-managed identities, and AI agents often have persistent privileges, unclear ownership, and weaker governance than human accounts [record_id:2572] [record_id:2620] [record_id:2817].
- **Agentic AI exposes an “intent gap.”** Existing IAM can accurately establish which agent holds a credential while remaining unable to tell whether its current action reflects the user’s intent or an injected instruction. Proposed responses include attenuating capability tokens, signed intent attestations, call-site policy, continuous trust, agent identity, and tool-level enforcement [record_id:2370] [record_id:2735] [record_id:2738] [record_id:3102].

Overall, the corpus argues for moving from one-time identity verification toward continuous, context-aware authorization with narrow delegation, strong lifecycle management, verifiable actor and message identity, robust session protection, and explicit controls over what authorized systems may do.

## Research Landscape

The collection is dominated by conference abstracts from Black Hat, DEF CON, BSides Las Vegas, CAMLIS, and AI-focused events between 2017 and 2026. Most records are offensive-security talks, red-team case studies, vulnerability disclosures, tool demonstrations, or proposed architectures. A smaller but significant subset covers authentication analytics, fraud detection, governance, deployment guidance, and defensive validation.

The research area spans several overlapping layers:

1. **Enterprise identity infrastructure**, especially Active Directory, Kerberos, Entra ID, certificate services, group policy, managed service accounts, hybrid identity, and endpoint credential stores.
2. **OAuth, OIDC, and cloud delegation**, including consent, token validation, API connectors, token stores, workload identity federation, managed identities, and cross-tenant trust.
3. **Authentication mechanisms**, including passwords, MFA, passkeys, WebAuthn/FIDO2, account recovery, device enrollment, biometrics, attestation, SMS OTP, and physical badges.
4. **Session and token security**, including cookies, PRTs, browser extensions, adversary-in-the-middle phishing, Browser-in-the-Middle systems, and infostealers.
5. **Identity detection and analytics**, using graph models, temporal point processes, risk-based authentication, behavioral baselines, threat-informed labeling, and deception.
6. **Non-human and agent identity**, where OAuth delegation and machine credentials meet prompt injection, autonomous action, and multi-agent orchestration.

The chronology suggests a shift in emphasis. Earlier CAMLIS material concentrates on modeling authentication behavior and detecting malicious sign-ins [record_id:246] [record_id:175]. The 2025 records broaden into passkeys, token theft, hybrid identity, browser extensions, cloud-managed identities, and connector abuse. The 2026 records increasingly treat identity as an autonomous delegation problem: how to identify agents, constrain their authority, propagate intent, and distinguish valid-but-malicious tool use from legitimate operation [record_id:2735] [record_id:2806] [record_id:3102] [record_id:3110].

Most evidence is supplied as talk descriptions rather than papers, code, datasets, or independently reproduced findings. Thus, the corpus is strong at identifying research directions and reported techniques, but uneven in evidentiary depth. Records with measurements, named CVEs, cross-vendor samples, concrete architectures, or specified evaluation counts are stronger than abstracts promising future demonstrations.

## Major Themes And Trends

### Authentication success is not sufficient authorization

A persistent theme is that applications confuse “this user authenticated” with “this user is entitled to this application or object.” One Entra ID study reports a tenant and authorization misconfiguration that permitted a researcher’s personal Microsoft account to access more than 20 internal Microsoft applications, including sensitive administrative functions [record_id:20]. GraphQL and REST API failures exposed private messages and media because object-level access controls were missing or inconsistent [record_id:2018]. A dealer platform reportedly allowed creation of a national administrator, user impersonation, ownership reassignment, and remote vehicle control, bypassing the practical value of 2FA [record_id:2036].

The same pattern appears in less obvious “credential” forms. Private email ingestion addresses can function as combined authentication and authorization tokens even though users and systems handle them as public identifiers [record_id:2774]. Gmail address canonicalization can cause password resets, login links, and recovery messages to reach the wrong person when external services treat dotted variants differently [record_id:2776]. In physical systems, badge readers, controllers, wiring, and badge issuance determine whether possession of a credential actually represents an authorized person [record_id:2125].

Automated vulnerability discovery is beginning to target this class directly. The “Auth-by-One” work uses strict validators for successful login and protected-resource access to let AI agents search for and validate authentication and authorization flaws in production applications [record_id:2326]. This is notable because bespoke authorization logic has historically required human reasoning; reliable validators may make systematic discovery more scalable.

### OAuth’s standardized core is undermined by proprietary composition

Several records emphasize that OAuth’s risks often arise not from the base protocol but from how platforms compose it.

Connection-based OAuth systems used by agentic AI and low-code integration platforms introduce cloud token stores and proprietary “OAuth connections.” These abstractions simplify re-delegation but reportedly reintroduce session fixation, open redirect, confused deputy, XSS, and cross-window communication vulnerabilities. The affected architectural pattern is described across Microsoft Azure, Power Platform, Copilot Studio, Composio, and ByteDance Coze [record_id:3]. Azure API Connections similarly combine ARM, API Management, custom connectors, consent servers, and token stores; reported exploitation progressed from low privilege to cross-tenant Key Vault secret access and arbitrary actions in connected services [record_id:15].

Traditional OAuth/OIDC implementation mistakes remain active: missing `state`, unsafe discovery-document handling, weak JWT validation, and replayable tokens can yield impersonation and authentication bypass [record_id:2383]. A cross-vendor healthcare study reports that seven of fourteen FHIR OAuth stacks disclosed whether a client ID existed through error discrepancies. The strongest part of that result is a 100-endpoint breadth test in which 98 hospital endpoints returned byte-identical response classes, supporting the claim that at least one issue was product-level rather than a site-specific configuration [record_id:2723].

Attackers are also shifting from phishing authentication to phishing authorization. Consent phishing, device-code phishing, and “ConsentFix” induce victims to authenticate legitimately at the real identity provider and then grant or deliver a token. Because no fake password form or MFA challenge is defeated, stronger login factors do not directly solve this attack [record_id:2775]. The corpus therefore treats consent screens, client registration, redirect handling, device-code workflows, and token audience validation as security boundaries in their own right.

### Delegated cloud identities create systemic confused deputies

Managed identities and cloud service agents frequently possess privileges that their callers do not. When a service exposes an operation without preserving caller-level authorization, the service becomes a privilege-escalation deputy.

Reported examples include Azure Automation flaws enabling cross-tenant managed-identity takeover [record_id:2672], managed-identity chains across Azure and GCP that let low-privileged users exercise organization-level service privileges [record_id:2684], and GCP service-agent designs that reportedly permit escalation from minimal access to project control [record_id:2715]. Google Cloud Identity-Aware Proxy misconfigurations can expose data despite no public IPs, VPC Service Controls, and hardened network perimeters, illustrating that identity policy—not network reachability—is the effective boundary [record_id:2495].

Workload Identity Federation represents both an improvement and a new persistence surface. It removes static GCP keys but allows external OIDC or X.509 identities to impersonate service accounts. Open pools, broad bindings, provider updates, and attacker-controlled certificate authorities can establish keyless persistence. Detection is complicated because credential-use telemetry may require paid logs that many teams do not enable [record_id:2787].

The AWS ECS-cape attack provides a related infrastructure example: a low-privileged task can reportedly exploit an undocumented ECS protocol to hijack IAM privileges from higher-privileged workloads on the same host [record_id:38]. CloudShell research shows that browser authentication, OAuth tokens, role sessions, WebSockets, persistent home directories, and default account entitlements can collectively turn free administrative shells into resilient execution and exfiltration infrastructure [record_id:2809] [record_id:2888].

### Active Directory remains a high-value identity control plane

Despite the cloud focus, Active Directory and Kerberos remain central. The records repeatedly demonstrate that obscure attributes, inherited permissions, administrative middleware, and protocol edge cases can still lead to domain compromise.

Hybrid AD-to-Entra research describes undocumented flows that allow lateral movement from on-premises AD into Entra ID, including techniques that bypass authentication or MFA and leave little useful logging [record_id:76]. Group Policy Objects can function as an attacker-controlled command-and-control mechanism for enumeration, persistence, lateral movement, and privilege escalation [record_id:1929]. Windows Server Failover Clusters add cluster credentials, Kerberos manipulation, and over-privileged cluster objects to the identity attack surface [record_id:10].

BadSuccessor abuses delegated Managed Service Accounts introduced in Windows Server 2025. A common permission can allegedly cause a domain controller to issue Kerberos tickets for arbitrary principals and expose NTLM hashes, even where dMSAs are not in active use [record_id:1966] [record_id:2547]. Later records report KerberLoss and ResetNightmare, identity-confusion flaws allowing Kerberos-to-NTLM downgrade, denial of service, and compromise of arbitrary accounts [record_id:2647] [record_id:2896]. Certificate Services research proposes ESC18–ESC22, five new escalation primitives reportedly validated against a fully patched Windows Server 2025 enterprise CA [record_id:2885].

The records also highlight neglected identity residue. Disabled and deleted accounts may retain dangerous permissions, nested group relationships, AdminSDHolder artifacts, or recoverable privilege paths. LazarusWakeUp is proposed to enumerate these risks beyond conventional BloodHound coverage [record_id:2982]. At a broader management-plane level, “Administrative Living-off-the-Land” uses legitimate CI/CD, IAM, cloud-control, and security-tool APIs so that attacker activity resembles normal administration [record_id:2718].

### Passkeys improve the ceremony but not the entire account lifecycle

The passkey records do not reject WebAuthn’s cryptography; rather, they argue that the ceremony is only one component of account security.

CTRAPS identifies client impersonation and API confusion at the CTAP layer, including credential erasure, factory resets, and user tracking against authenticators such as YubiKeys [record_id:1992]. Another attack proxies WebAuthn API calls through a malicious extension or injected client-side script to forge registration and authentication responses where relying parties omit attestation or metadata checks [record_id:1997]. “Pass-the-Passkey” reports implementation flaws analogous to Pass-the-Hash and NTLM relay, including reuse of stored signatures to impersonate privileged users while bypassing phishing-resistant MFA [record_id:2590].

Synced passkeys may be phishable through surrounding synchronization and recovery workflows [record_id:2080]. Browser-extension clickjacking can expose credentials, TOTP values, card details, and in some cases passkey operations with a single user interaction [record_id:1851]. Android key-attestation parsing flaws show that a hardware-backed key claim is only as reliable as certificate-chain parsing and backend validation [record_id:74].

The broadest synthesis argues that deployments fail in storage, synchronization, recovery, alternate login methods, relying-party validation, and downgrade paths even when the WebAuthn ceremony is correct. It reports that every relying party in one audit had at least one server-side weakness and introduces a Burp extension for manipulating passkey traffic [record_id:2945]. A more introductory record reflects the operational confusion teams experience when adopting passkeys [record_id:2839]. Account recovery is therefore essential: testing 25 popular websites produced an account-recovery audit suite and design recommendations for the passwordless future [record_id:70].

### Biometrics and hardware roots of trust have fragile boundaries

Biometric records repeatedly question where matching occurs, what is trusted, and whether client-side controls can be manipulated.

Reverse engineering of Windows Hello for Business reportedly found that a local administrator could replace facial biometric templates and authenticate as another enrolled user, potentially enabling domain-admin lateral movement [record_id:45]. Later work expands fingerprint assessment across Windows, Linux, and macOS, focusing on sensor-to-host protocols rather than physical fingerprint spoofs [record_id:2654]. Mobile banking and KYC research reports bypasses of root detection, RASP, liveness checks, selfie capture, and synthetic-face detection through kernel tools and Frida-based media manipulation [record_id:2884] [record_id:2909]. An EEG-based authentication proposal offers dynamic brain signals as a lightweight IoT identity mechanism, but the record supplies a concept rather than a mature security evaluation [record_id:1932].

Hardware attestation is also questioned. A low-cost FPGA implementation can reportedly impersonate a Windows-compatible TPM, challenging assumptions that a platform root of trust is difficult to counterfeit [record_id:2915]. At the telecommunications boundary, Kannel gateway vulnerabilities could censor or manipulate SMS messages that carry OTPs, demonstrating that even a correctly generated factor depends on untrusted delivery infrastructure [record_id:2906].

### Sessions and tokens are the practical target after MFA

The corpus repeatedly frames post-authentication artifacts as more valuable than passwords.

Reverse-proxy phishing kits can proxy an entire MFA flow and steal resulting cookies. Threat-informed DNS labeling and authentication analysis reportedly produced better supervised classifiers than unsupervised anomaly detection for this problem [record_id:156]. On macOS, weaknesses in Microsoft’s Intune Company Portal SSO implementation reportedly permit PRT cookie extraction and device registration using a token carrying an MFA claim, turning short-lived theft into durable persistence [record_id:1987].

Chrome’s App-Bound encryption raises the cost of cookie theft but remains bypassable under described conditions; Device Bound Session Cookies are proposed as a stronger future control [record_id:2543]. A broader session-security review likewise presents device-bound credentials as potentially transformative if widely adopted [record_id:2570]. Infostealer-focused records show why storage encryption alone is insufficient once the user execution boundary is compromised: browser data, DPAPI keys, LSASS material, autofill, cookies, and extension secrets become available together [record_id:2818] [record_id:2840].

Phishing systems continue to evolve around these defenses. Microsoft’s legitimate login domain can allegedly be repurposed for credential collection through legitimate functionality [record_id:1960]. Browser-in-the-Middle designs render the real application in an attacker-controlled browser, harvest post-MFA tokens, and avoid conventional proxy rewriting problems [record_id:2859]. Persistent BitM extends this to keystroke capture, file replacement, browser-device access, and post-logout persistence [record_id:2942].

### Passwords remain important, but policy and attack models are changing

The records do not support declaring passwords obsolete. Instead, they distinguish poor password policy from the continued practical value of password attacks.

Large-scale cracking work reports recovery of 92% of 936 million password hashes and discusses hardware, wordlists, and CPU/GPU tradeoffs [record_id:2417]. Introductory and specialist Hashcat talks cover standard workflows, custom modules, and brainwallet cracking [record_id:2765] [record_id:1961]. Token-based Markov generation using GPT-2 BPE units reportedly outperforms OMEN across 14 leak corpora and substantially improves recovery of enterprise-style passwords [record_id:2924].

Forced password expiry is challenged with enterprise datasets suggesting that users mutate old passwords, reduce effective entropy, and create predictable patterns; proposed alternatives include event-driven rotation and time-to-crack thresholds [record_id:2500]. Hidden SaaS identities outside corporate SSO remain unmanaged and exposed to reuse and sharing [record_id:2418]. Old breach credentials continue to enable new campaigns because identity data is repeatedly aggregated and enriched [record_id:2814]. Meanwhile, synthetic high-entropy passwords can be used as attack noise to evade reuse-focused controls and obscure genuine credential attempts [record_id:2556].

### Agentic AI transforms identity into an intent and delegation problem

The newest and most distinctive trend is the claim that existing identity-based authorization cannot adequately constrain autonomous agents.

Indirect prompt injection can cause agents to use legitimate permissions against their users. A Google Calendar invitation reportedly triggered Gemini agents to delete events, exfiltrate data, operate smart-home devices, and propagate between agents and devices [record_id:53]. AI-driven KYC extraction pipelines can be manipulated by document-embedded instructions into cross-record reads and writes, despite schema and compliance controls [record_id:2328]. Enterprise Copilot can amplify attacker-controlled content and turn device-code phishing instructions into trusted-looking organizational guidance [record_id:3107].

Several records explicitly reframe prompt injection as authorization failure. Bearer tokens convey who may act, but not why an action was initiated; the proposed remedy combines call-site policy, signed intent attestations, and architectural human approval [record_id:2735]. Capability-based authorization instead gives agents ephemeral, attenuating, holder-bound warrants that constrain each delegation hop [record_id:2370]. Another practical architecture combines Auth0 machine-to-machine scopes with decentralized identifiers and signed ATProto messages to give agents verifiable identity and message authorship [record_id:2806].

Other records emphasize operational controls: capability bounding, memory isolation, prompt-injection tuning, and OAuth device flow for headless agents [record_id:2359]; ownership and observability for autonomous “synthetic insiders” [record_id:2817]; and continuous trust that asks whether the current human or machine actor remains appropriate, not merely who logged in [record_id:2738]. Policy analysis similarly proposes an agent identity standard, intent monitoring, and behavioral audit requirements [record_id:2597].

There is disagreement over how much can be solved through IAM. One position argues that conventional scopes, continuous verification, and behavioral baselines cannot detect semantic compromise when every action remains authorized [record_id:3102]. SADF adds nuance: in 2,656 runs, orchestration frameworks produced materially different compromise rates, while delegated-authority attacks sometimes failed because model safety training appeared to recognize malicious intent even after authorization checks passed [record_id:3110]. This suggests that cryptographic authorization and model-level safety may be complementary rather than interchangeable.

## Methods, Tools, And Approaches Discussed

The records describe both offensive and defensive workflows.

For **OAuth and API assessment**, common methods include intercepting authorization flows, mutating redirect and discovery parameters, replaying JWTs, checking state and audience validation, comparing error responses, and fuzzing token parsers. OWASP ZAP is used for token interception and replay [record_id:2383]. FHIRbug packages a six-phase healthcare API methodology into 22 modules and 14 command-line functions [record_id:2723]. AI-assisted auth testing uses explicit AuthN and AuthZ validators to determine whether an agent has actually logged in or crossed a protected boundary [record_id:2326].

For **Active Directory**, the collection discusses Kerberoasting, NTLM relay, unconstrained delegation, MachineAccountQuota abuse, LDAP signing, Windows Event Forwarding, GPO enumeration, dMSA abuse, ADCS exploitation, and analysis of forgotten principals [record_id:2388] [record_id:1929] [record_id:2547] [record_id:2885] [record_id:2982]. RPC-Racer races trusted services to register attacker-controlled RPC endpoints and can induce privileged processes to authenticate machine accounts to an attacker-selected server [record_id:2016]. OverLAPS combines PDB symbols, static analysis, Frida, and Microsoft Detours to intercept and manipulate Windows LAPS behavior [record_id:2082].

For **passkeys and device trust**, researchers use malicious browser extensions, client-side API proxying, CTAP manipulation over USB and NFC, Proxmark3, Android applications, attestation-chain parsing, Burp extensions, and fuzzing of relying-party validation [record_id:1992] [record_id:1997] [record_id:2590] [record_id:2945]. Biometric work uses driver and firmware reverse engineering, protocol tracing, Frida hooks, controlled YUV buffer replacement, and synthetic face generation [record_id:2654] [record_id:2884].

For **credential and session attacks**, methods include Evilginx-style reverse proxies, browser-process instrumentation, cookie decryption, DPAPI and LSASS extraction, Chrome DevTools Protocol automation, MHTML snapshots, and persistent attacker-hosted browsers [record_id:156] [record_id:2543] [record_id:2840] [record_id:2859] [record_id:2942]. Browser extension research examines requested permissions, content scripts, UI overlays, and clickjacking against password managers [record_id:1851] [record_id:2438].

For **cloud and non-human identity**, environment graphing is prominent. SecBridge, Cartography, and AWSPX visualize IAM relationships and attack paths [record_id:2484]. NHI-Hound is intended to discover and graph service accounts, API keys, OAuth applications, Kubernetes identities, CI/CD secrets, and AI-agent credentials [record_id:2620]. CloudBasher automates private CloudShell protocols, environment creation, persistence, distributed execution, and exfiltration [record_id:2809] [record_id:2888]. AWS Threat Hunter builds individualized behavioral baselines for IAM principals rather than relying solely on generic event rules [record_id:2813].

Several records promote **secretless or short-lived workload authentication**. AWS IRSA and Azure Workload Identity are demonstrated across Kubernetes clusters without manually managed static secrets [record_id:2396]. Private PKI deployment on inexpensive YubiKey-class HSMs provides protected offline root signing and constrained trust anchors [record_id:2409]. Defensive guidance for non-human identities emphasizes ownership, lifecycle management, least privilege, governance, and moving beyond long-lived credentials [record_id:2468] [record_id:2572].

For **analytics and detection**, methods include:

- risk-based authentication evaluated against opportunistic, targeted, and advanced attacks [record_id:126];
- heterogeneous graph neural networks for Azure sign-in classification [record_id:175];
- graph-based user-resource analytics for insider risk and overprivilege [record_id:154];
- point-process modeling of temporal authentication dependencies [record_id:246];
- TF-IDF similarity over transaction, device, and behavioral metadata [record_id:78];
- hierarchical spill-tree approximations for credential misuse and domain squatting [record_id:253];
- honeytokens planted where attackers are likely to search for credentials [record_id:2755].

For **AI-agent authorization**, proposed mechanisms include cryptographically attenuating capabilities inspired by Macaroons and UCAN [record_id:2370], signed intent across delegation hops [record_id:2735], decentralized identifiers and signed messages [record_id:2806], and execution-grounded evaluation rather than substring-based benchmark scoring [record_id:3110]. Adversarial red-versus-blue agent benchmarking runs autonomous attackers and investigators against production-like multi-forest AD environments, using attacker execution traces as ground truth [record_id:2666].

## Notable Talks, Records, And Evidence

The most representative OAuth record is the connection-based architecture analysis, because it identifies a structural shift from application-managed OAuth flows to proprietary cloud token services and links that shift to classic web vulnerability classes [record_id:3]. The Azure API Connections study complements it with a concrete cross-tenant secret-leak path [record_id:15], while the Entra internal-application case illustrates the persistent difference between authenticating an account and authorizing that account for a tenant or application [record_id:20].

The healthcare FHIR study is among the strongest cross-vendor records because it gives a sample size, a defined weakness, multiple discriminator patterns, and a product-level breadth test [record_id:2723]. The SADF record is similarly notable for reporting 2,656 runs, differentiating raw substring scores from execution-grounded compromise, and isolating orchestration framework choice as an independent variable [record_id:3110].

BadSuccessor is important because it turns a new defensive account type into an escalation primitive and appears in substantively identical DEF CON and BSides records [record_id:1966] [record_id:2547]. KerberLoss and ResetNightmare are another duplicated conference presentation with named CVEs and a promised automated exploit tool [record_id:2647] [record_id:2896]. These records are high-impact but still derive from presenter descriptions rather than independent reproduction in the supplied material.

The passkey corpus is unusually broad. CTRAPS targets the authenticator protocol [record_id:1992]; passkey proxying targets client-side APIs [record_id:1997]; Pass-the-Passkey targets implementation and replay-like weaknesses [record_id:2590]; and the 2026 attack-surface synthesis examines downgrade, recovery, storage, and relying-party failures around the ceremony [record_id:2945]. Collectively, these records support a nuanced conclusion: WebAuthn can be cryptographically sound while a passkey-enabled account remains vulnerable.

The macOS PRT study is representative of identity persistence research because it connects endpoint process validation, token extraction, MFA claims, and device registration [record_id:1987]. The authorization-phishing record is equally important for defenders because it explains why passkeys and other phishing-resistant login factors do not stop a victim from legitimately granting an attacker OAuth access [record_id:2775].

For non-human identity, “Ghost Credentials” provides the most complete offensive lifecycle: discover, validate, assume, expand, and persist across code history, container layers, metadata services, CI/CD, SaaS, and AI integrations [record_id:2620]. The managed-identity confused-deputy analysis generalizes the issue across Azure and GCP rather than treating each case as an isolated cloud bug [record_id:2684].

The strongest agentic-identity debate appears across three records. Capability warrants seek to make escalation cryptographically impossible beyond a delegated bound [record_id:2370]. The intent-attestation proposal argues that authorization must convey why an action exists [record_id:2735]. The Zero Trust critique demonstrates the residual problem: an injected agent may exfiltrate through fully authorized APIs while every identity and scope check passes [record_id:3102]. Together they define a central open research question: which aspects of agent intent can be represented and enforced deterministically?

Finally, the empirical authentication-detection records provide valuable balance to the offensive material. Risk-based authentication is evaluated in production 2FA [record_id:126]; threat-informed labeling improves reverse-proxy phishing classification [record_id:156]; and heterogeneous graphs model users, devices, services, and authentication events jointly [record_id:175]. These records suggest that identity defense increasingly requires relational and temporal context rather than isolated login rules.

## Gaps, Limits, And Open Questions

The largest limitation is **source form**. Most records are promotional conference abstracts. Claims of complete bypass, cross-tenant compromise, universal vulnerability, or 100% success are not accompanied by full datasets, patch histories, exploit constraints, or independent validation. Named CVEs and promised tools improve traceability, but do not themselves establish reproducibility.

Several future-facing 2026 records describe talks or tools that may not yet have been publicly delivered. Their statements should be treated as reported research plans or presenter claims, not confirmed outcomes. This especially matters for claims such as every tested banking application failing [record_id:2884], a 100% biometric-framework bypass rate [record_id:2909], and newly proposed ADCS escalation categories [record_id:2885].

The corpus also leaves several technical questions unresolved:

- How should intent be encoded without creating a new spoofable metadata layer?
- Can signed intent survive multi-agent delegation, asynchronous workflows, retries, and partial failure?
- Who is authorized to issue, attenuate, revoke, or reinterpret an agent’s capability?
- How should tools evaluate runtime constraints that cannot be encoded in an offline capability token?
- What telemetry distinguishes malicious use of a managed identity from legitimate service operation when API calls are identical?
- How can session-bound credentials be deployed across browsers and identity providers without breaking recovery, roaming, or accessibility?
- What are the real-world false-positive and user-friction costs of risk-based authentication and continuous trust?
- Can passkey recovery and synchronization be secured without recreating password-equivalent fallback paths?
- How should organizations inventory SaaS, service, and AI identities whose ownership is distributed across development teams and vendors?
- Which cloud identity logs should be mandatory or free, especially where use telemetry is currently placed in paid tiers [record_id:2787]?

There is limited comparative analysis of revocation speed, token lifetime, delegated-access review, or recovery after identity-provider compromise. Incident records show progression from help-desk phishing to IdP, GitHub, and AWS compromise [record_id:2599], but the corpus gives less detail on containment sequencing and restoration of trust.

Human factors appear, but less systematically than technical exploitation. Account recovery, fake personas, phishing persuasion, and confusing consent interfaces are present, yet there is little controlled evidence about which interface changes or training methods consistently reduce compromise. AI-assisted social engineering may reduce the cost of personalization [record_id:2192], but the corpus does not quantify long-term victim resistance or attacker adaptation.

Finally, the records rarely distinguish vulnerabilities inherent to a standard from failures in a specific implementation, deployment, or surrounding workflow. The passkey material is better than most at making this distinction; other areas would benefit from the same separation.
