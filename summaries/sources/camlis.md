# Topic: Source: camlis

## Meta-Summary

The CAMLIS corpus traces the development of machine learning for cybersecurity from 2017 through 2025. Its earliest records concentrate on representation learning, anomaly detection, malware classification, network analytics, SOC workflow, and the infrastructure needed to process security data at scale. Over time, the agenda expands into adversarial machine learning, model robustness, explainability, autonomous cyber defense, LLM-assisted security operations, and—especially in 2023–2025—prompt injection, jailbreaks, backdoors, software-supply-chain risks, and operational AI red teaming.

Several persistent conclusions emerge. First, the practical difficulty of cybersecurity ML lies as much in labels, datasets, evaluation design, feature semantics, changing distributions, and analyst integration as in model architecture. Second, context is repeatedly more useful than isolated events: graphs, event sequences, multi-turn conversations, process ancestry, infrastructure relationships, and temporal behavior all help expose activity that appears benign when examined one record at a time. Third, systems optimized only for average accuracy are inadequate where false positives and false negatives have asymmetric operational costs. Finally, increasingly capable agents amplify both defensive automation and attack surfaces, making bounded execution, provenance, human approval, lifecycle red teaming, and system-level threat models central concerns.

The evidence consists primarily of conference abstracts and presentation summaries rather than full experimental artifacts. Many records report concrete datasets, metrics, architectures, or case studies, but independent replication details are generally absent. A small number have empty or unavailable raw text and therefore contribute little beyond showing that a session existed.

## Research Landscape

CAMLIS is primarily a machine-learning-for-security venue rather than a general security conference. Across the corpus, the dominant record types are research-paper abstracts, practitioner presentations, demonstrations, tutorials, and keynote descriptions. The work spans enterprise SOCs, malware laboratories, identity providers, cloud platforms, operational technology, vulnerability management, threat intelligence, and AI safety.

The chronology shows a clear evolution:

- **2017–2019** emphasizes basic representation, feature engineering, clustering, large-scale data pipelines, malware detection, network anomaly detection, and early adversarial-ML concerns. Researchers debate raw-byte learning versus expert features and begin treating red-team exercises as sources of labeled data.
- **2021–2022** places greater emphasis on large benchmark datasets, campaign reconstruction, analyst-facing summarization, uncertainty, concept drift, weak-label evaluation, identity analytics, and staged analysis that escalates difficult samples to more expensive tools.
- **2023–2024** brings LLMs into both security operations and the attack surface. LLMs are benchmarked as analyst assistants, used for vulnerability analysis and attribution, attacked through prompt injection or extraction, and defended using activation analysis, input provenance techniques, and backdoor mitigation.
- **2025** is distinctly agentic and system-oriented. The records discuss adaptive multi-turn jailbreaks, multimodal prompt injection, graph-level LLM backdoors, computer-use agents, autonomous scam calls, scalable AI red-team infrastructure, executable adversary simulation, and LLM-assisted cloud or code-security workflows.

The corpus is broad but not evenly distributed. Enterprise detection, SOC analytics, and malware analysis form the largest long-running bodies of work. Identity, privacy, OT, vulnerability prioritization, and governance appear regularly but in smaller numbers. The newest records shift heavily toward generative-AI security, while retaining older CAMLIS concerns about reproducibility, realistic data, operational cost, and human oversight.

## Major Themes And Trends

### Contextual and relational detection

A recurring argument is that isolated alerts or static feature vectors omit the relationships needed to understand an attack. Graph-based work models Kubernetes sessions as event graphs [record_id:153], enterprise users and resources as a bipartite insider-risk graph [record_id:154], Azure sign-ins as heterogeneous authentication graphs [record_id:175], and malware as nodes connected to domains, URLs, and infrastructure [record_id:217]. Process ancestry similarly reveals suspicious “living off the land” chains that individual executables do not [record_id:221].

This relational trend also appears in campaign analysis. Pairwise similarity is used to associate heterogeneous network logs [record_id:193]; HeAT reconstructs activity leading to critical indicators [record_id:196]; CLEAR-ROAD mines temporally co-occurring alerts around rare critical events [record_id:208]; and dynamic-graph summarization extracts the core sequence and entities from incident reports [record_id:197]. Newer work applies GraphRAG ideas to cloud configurations, converting them into security-enriched knowledge graphs for natural-language reasoning [record_id:118].

The same principle reappears in generative-AI security. Multi-turn attacks distribute malicious intent across a conversation rather than exposing it in one prompt [record_id:112], scam agents use memory and subgoals to sustain deception [record_id:131], and computer-use agents introduce perception–reasoning–action loops in which ambient UI content can influence privileged behavior [record_id:127]. Across these domains, the corpus consistently warns against evaluating events, prompts, or actions independently of their trajectory and environment.

### Malware ML: from handcrafted features to structured representation learning

Malware analysis is the corpus’s deepest continuous research area. Early work applies CNNs directly to raw bytes [record_id:262], opcode and operand embeddings [record_id:233], graph integration [record_id:228], and semi-supervised file representations [record_id:239]. Other work uses image-style malware representations and Grad-CAM explanations [record_id:218], byte-model activation analysis tied back to executable semantics [record_id:238], or self-supervised convolutional autoencoders [record_id:176].

The corpus does not converge on a simple “deep learning replaces experts” conclusion. Raw-byte approaches can reduce parser and feature-engineering costs, but several records find that domain structure remains valuable. Non-rectangular filters are motivated by executable shape [record_id:222]; structure-aware vision transformers use PE sections to match production-grade traditional classifiers [record_id:141]; shell-specific preprocessing dramatically outperforms generic NLP tokenization [record_id:191]; and security-aware encoders for IPs, domains, usernames, and URLs outperform naïve encodings [record_id:224]. Collectively, these works suggest a continuum between fully handcrafted and fully learned representations, with lightweight semantic hints often providing the best operational trade-off.

Malware research also broadens from binary conviction to analyst enrichment. Semantic tags describe capabilities and properties rather than only families [record_id:219]; metric embeddings support detection, family classification, and attribute transfer [record_id:172]; StringSifter ranks analyst-relevant strings using weak supervision [record_id:229]; neural translation predicts meaningful names for disassembled functions [record_id:207]; and MalDICT introduces behavior, platform, exploitation, and packer datasets rather than limiting evaluation to benign/malicious or family labels [record_id:160].

### Dataset quality, labels, and realistic evaluation

CAMLIS repeatedly treats data engineering as a first-class scientific problem. Large public resources include SOREL-20M [record_id:195], BETH [record_id:194], PEVuln [record_id:140], MalDICT [record_id:160], EMBER updates [record_id:226], and community-hosted collections through the Splunk Datasets Project [record_id:236]. Other presentations explain scalable malware-labeling infrastructure, historical label rollback, and multi-feed reconciliation [record_id:214].

Several records show why dataset size alone is insufficient. Antivirus labels change over time [record_id:240], antivirus-engine consensus is volatile [record_id:200], and C/C++ vulnerability datasets contain major duplication and train/test leakage [record_id:198]. Academic malware benchmarks may be too small or easy to reveal operationally meaningful effect sizes, motivating harder, configurable train/test splits [record_id:158]. Weak or noisy labels motivate marker-based comparative evaluation [record_id:184], weak supervision [record_id:229], and threat-informed mining of rare positive authentication examples [record_id:156].

Synthetic and emulated data are common responses to label scarcity. Adversary simulations provide labeled attack telemetry [record_id:237]; web-shell episodes are manufactured and overlaid on enterprise traffic [record_id:174]; an agent-based simulator executes approved attack plans against controlled infrastructure [record_id:130]; and Simula-generated data plus active learning support a self-sustaining enterprise detector [record_id:125]. The corpus nevertheless recognizes that simulated behavior can introduce bias and may not represent adaptive real-world attackers.

### Operational metrics, uncertainty, and human judgment

The records repeatedly reject accuracy as a sufficient operational objective. F1 treats false positives and false negatives symmetrically, while Cscore introduces an explicit cost ratio and reports substantial average savings [record_id:142]. Earlier work similarly addresses asymmetric website-classification mistakes through cost-cluster auxiliary losses [record_id:245]. Multi-stage classifiers optimize both decision quality and the cost of forwarding samples to deeper analysis [record_id:251], while uncertainty-driven deferral decides when expensive malware analysis is justified [record_id:173].

Uncertainty and out-of-distribution behavior receive sustained attention. Bayesian approximations are proposed for classifier uncertainty [record_id:248]; hybrid discriminative-generative malware models attempt to identify unsupported samples and concept drift [record_id:202]; and black-box adversarial queries are monitored as distributional drift [record_id:211]. Firenze explicitly uses weak domain signals and statistical hypothesis tests where trustworthy labels are unavailable [record_id:184].

Human involvement remains central even in highly automated systems. LLM-based attribution improves when analysts add contemporaneous threat-actor relevance [record_id:137]. Analyst-facing explanation interfaces expose model decisions and relevant features [record_id:244]. Alert-clustering systems preserve final analyst authority while allowing group-level decisions [record_id:192]. Executable adversary simulations require review before actions run [record_id:130]. The overall position is not anti-automation; it is that human expertise should shape objectives, labels, escalation, explanations, and safety boundaries.

### Adversarial ML and the expansion to generative-AI security

Adversarial machine learning appears throughout the archive. Early examples include attacks on tree ensembles [record_id:249], backdoor poisoning of malware classifiers [record_id:227], static PE evasion and representation-consistency regularization [record_id:220], Meterpreter perturbation experiments [record_id:213], and ransomware red-team programs that expose behavioral detector weaknesses [record_id:230]. Later work attacks phishing-page detectors with rendering-preserving HTML changes [record_id:165], trading policies [record_id:203], perceptual-hash scanning [record_id:212], financial-reporting models [record_id:124], and model explanations themselves [record_id:204].

From 2023 onward, LLM security becomes prominent. Repeated control-character sequences are reported as a prompt-injection technique [record_id:170], and a tutorial distinguishes user prompt injection from cross-domain prompt injection [record_id:171]. Spotlighting marks untrusted input provenance and reports a large reduction in indirect-prompt-injection success [record_id:144]. Residual-stream activation analysis classifies adversarial prompts under white-box access [record_id:148]. Targeted neuron noising suppresses Trojan activations while attempting to preserve utility [record_id:150].

The 2025 records intensify this attacker–defender cycle. HarmNet constructs and refines adaptive multi-turn jailbreaks [record_id:112], while LLM salting rotates refusal directions to invalidate reusable jailbreak prompts [record_id:113]. ShadowLogic inserts secret-triggered backdoors by modifying computational graphs rather than only model weights or training data [record_id:114]. Text2VLM converts text evaluation data into multimodal formats and reports greater susceptibility when visual inputs carry typographic injection [record_id:115].

The red-team records argue that testing must mature operationally. PyRIT provides reusable targets, converters, datasets, scorers, memory, and orchestration [record_id:128], building on its earlier model- and platform-agnostic framework description [record_id:135]. BlackIce packages fragmented testing tools in a reproducible container [record_id:129]. A broader critique argues that red teaming must cover data, interfaces, governance, infrastructure, maintenance, and retirement rather than reducing the practice to jailbreak prompting [record_id:133].

### Agents as both defensive systems and new attack surfaces

Autonomous defense is present from the earliest keynote vision of host-based “bonware” [record_id:231] through explained, hierarchical reinforcement-learning defenders [record_id:185]. Maritime OT work compares multi-agent reinforcement-learning schemes in a simulated platform-management system [record_id:163], while Co-Decyber decomposes GPS-spoofing defense into cooperative decisions [record_id:136]. Newer systems pursue topology-independent defense [record_id:116] and contextual policy changes based on mission objectives without retraining [record_id:120].

The same capabilities create offensive and safety risks. ScamAgent applies planning, deception templates, memory, adaptation, and speech generation to scam calls [record_id:131]. Computer-use agents can misread UIs, accept indirect instructions, leak reasoning, bypass approval, or convert prompt injection into conventional code execution [record_id:127]. Package hallucination creates a software-supply-chain opening when attackers register invented dependencies [record_id:132]. This dual use drives repeated calls for bounded execution, reauthentication, action provenance, virtualized downloads, human approval, and context-aware monitoring.

## Methods, Tools, And Approaches Discussed

Graph methods are among the most recurrent techniques. The records use GNN and relational-GCN embeddings for Kubernetes and authentication telemetry [record_id:153] [record_id:175], matrix factorization for insider-risk relationships [record_id:154], graph embeddings for malware infrastructure [record_id:217], PageRank-based integration for .NET code graphs [record_id:228], and threat-graph metric learning for personalized intelligence retrieval [record_id:145]. Graphs also underpin campaign visualization, cloud reasoning, and security-data schemas [record_id:197] [record_id:118] [record_id:189].

Sequence and temporal methods include point processes for authentication behavior [record_id:246], sticky HDP-HMMs for multimodal cyber-physical systems [record_id:186], DistilBERT for shell-session anomalies [record_id:169], LSTMs for homoglyph domains [record_id:177], and temporal co-occurrence mining for alerts [record_id:208]. Distributional NetFlow quantiles are used to detect botnet C2 hosts [record_id:179], while DIP-ECOD separates multimodal distributions before outlier scoring [record_id:139].

Reinforcement learning appears in defensive policy design, exfiltration modeling, adversarial malware transformation, and retrieval. Exfiltration is represented as an MDP incorporating payload and protocol choices [record_id:162]. Vulnerable-code discovery uses iterative query generation within an RL environment [record_id:122]. TERLA and contextual RL target generalization and mission-aware defense [record_id:116] [record_id:120]. Maritime and vehicle-platooning studies use cooperative or multi-agent policy learning [record_id:163] [record_id:136].

A consistent engineering pattern is **staged computation**. Cheap models defer uncertain files to expensive models [record_id:173]; lightweight binary emulation occupies a middle ground between static and sandbox analysis [record_id:206]; Binee aims to extract dynamic-like process information at static-analysis cost [record_id:225]; and test-time compute is evaluated for container-vulnerability and alert-triage agents [record_id:117]. URL content filtering distills an LLM into a much smaller inline model [record_id:166], while model leeching shows the same distillation concept can be weaponized for extraction [record_id:167].

LLM workflows increasingly combine planning, retrieval, tools, and verification. Container CVE analysis uses a plan-and-execute agent, RAG, SBOMs, source code, and VEX categorization [record_id:138]. Threat attribution combines LLM TTP extraction, embeddings, and a downstream attribution model [record_id:137]. RIG-RAG builds a cloud knowledge graph from configuration data [record_id:118]. Vulnerable-code discovery iteratively reasons, searches, retrieves, and repeats [record_id:122].

Infrastructure methods include immutable SQL-orchestrated pipelines over Snowflake and autoscaling compute [record_id:157], AWS data lakes and Redshift for historical malware labels [record_id:214], serverless binary-analysis pipelines [record_id:235], and containerized AI-testing environments [record_id:129]. Curl applies secure multiparty computation with wavelet-compressed lookup tables to private LLM inference [record_id:149], while AdapterSwap dynamically composes low-rank adapters to support continual learning, deletion, and access control [record_id:151].

## Notable Talks, Records, And Evidence

Several records are especially representative of CAMLIS’s trajectory:

- The raw-byte malware-classification work from 2017 establishes the long-running representation-learning agenda and directly compares learned byte features with expert features [record_id:262]. The 2024 structure-aware malware study revisits that debate at much larger production scale and finds that lightweight PE semantics can close the performance gap [record_id:141].
- SOREL-20M is notable for scale, high-quality labels, metadata, tags, disarmed samples, and released baselines [record_id:195]. MalDICT later expands the set of benchmark tasks beyond binary detection and family classification [record_id:160].
- The C/C++ dataset analysis offers unusually strong evidence about benchmark contamination, including major overlap caused by duplicated or augmented examples [record_id:198]. It is an important warning against interpreting high scores without dataset forensics.
- Spotlighting provides one of the corpus’s clearest quantified prompt-injection defenses, reporting a reduction from over 50% attack success to below 2% in its experiments [record_id:144]. Its evidence is still model- and setup-dependent, but it gives a concrete defensive hypothesis suitable for replication.
- The package-hallucination study evaluates multiple model families and languages, reports rates ranging from 0.22% to 46.15%, and links the behavior to a plausible package-repository exploitation path [record_id:132]. It is notable for connecting generative error to conventional supply-chain compromise.
- The computer-use-agent taxonomy is important because it unifies AI perception and delegation failures with clickjacking, file writes, browser installation, handler abuse, and code execution [record_id:127]. It captures the corpus’s broader shift from model-only testing to system threat modeling.
- The PyRIT presentations show maturation from a composable framework concept [record_id:135] to infrastructure reportedly used for multilingual, multimodal, million-conversation red-team evaluations [record_id:128]. The later talk is also valuable for explicitly documenting remaining gaps in scoring, UI-only targets, agentic systems, and human review.
- The “Red Teaming AI Red Teaming” presentation provides the corpus’s strongest methodological critique: mature testing should examine the whole lifecycle and the environment around an AI system, not only prompt-response behavior [record_id:133].
- The agent-based adversary simulator is notable for combining LLM planning, human approval, bounded infrastructure, actual execution, and telemetry generation [record_id:130]. It also reports materially different refusal behavior among models, illustrating that model choice affects both capability and safety.
- The web-shell case study is representative of operational rigor: threat emulation, real enterprise background traffic, connected-component object construction, iterative noise removal, model validation, and explainability are treated as one workflow rather than separate research tasks [record_id:174].

Evidence strength varies. Records with explicit sample counts, datasets, comparison methods, or measured results—such as PEVuln [record_id:140], DIP-ECOD [record_id:139], Cscore [record_id:142], SOREL-20M [record_id:195], the MFA-phishing study [record_id:156], and the adaptive honeypot experiment [record_id:159]—provide stronger empirical anchors than conceptual talks. Detailed presentation summaries also give stronger architectural evidence than one-sentence abstracts, although they remain secondary descriptions rather than full artifacts.

## Gaps, Limits, And Open Questions

The largest limitation is source form. Most entries are abstracts or presentation summaries. They often report favorable results without enough information about preprocessing, hyperparameters, confidence intervals, negative findings, code availability, or replication. Claims about “state of the art,” production equivalence, or high accuracy should therefore be treated as reported outcomes, not independently verified conclusions.

Generalization remains unresolved. Many models are evaluated on one vendor’s telemetry, proprietary enterprise data, controlled simulations, or a limited set of model APIs. It is unclear how well graph detectors transfer between organizations, how prompt-injection defenses survive adaptive attackers, or how autonomous defenders behave under distribution shift and incomplete observability. The archive repeatedly identifies this problem but rarely offers cross-organization replication.

The corpus also leaves important governance questions open. Human-in-the-loop approval is frequently recommended, but there is little comparative evidence about which approval designs prevent automation bias or approval fatigue. Similarly, cryptographic action tagging, lifecycle monitoring, model retirement, coordinated disclosure, and access-controlled knowledge adapters are proposed, but few records evaluate them as integrated production controls.

For generative AI, scoring is a major unresolved bottleneck. PyRIT’s large-scale workflows still require custom scorers and human review [record_id:128]. Multi-turn harms, indirect prompt injection, agent delegation, and UI attacks lack common benchmarks. It remains unclear how to measure attack success when malicious intent is distributed across many locally innocuous actions.

The malware literature has rich benchmark resources, but reproducible, temporally realistic evaluation is still difficult. Labels evolve, malware families drift, antivirus engines correlate, and random train/test splits can leak near-duplicates. Future work needs evaluation protocols that combine temporal separation, family separation, deduplication, adversarial manipulation, and operational cost.

Privacy, fairness, and civil-liberties implications receive less attention than detection performance. Exceptions include the ethical warning around demographic signals for fake-account detection [record_id:247], privacy attacks enabled by explanations [record_id:204], private inference through MPC [record_id:149], and the privacy implications of client-side perceptual-hash scanning [record_id:212]. More work is needed on how security analytics affect different user populations and how defensive telemetry can be collected proportionately.
